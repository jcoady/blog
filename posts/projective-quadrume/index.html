From Quadrea to Quadrume: Climbing a Dimension

In our earlier post on the projective quadrea, we watched N. J. Wildberger’s rational trigonometry do something remarkable: it took the metrical geometry of a projective triangle in \(\mathbb{P}^2\) — a triangle built from three lines through the origin of an ambient vector space \(\mathbb{V}^3\) — and expressed every measurement as a ratio of polynomials in a bilinear form. No \(\sin\), no \(\cos\), no square roots, no transcendental machinery. Just a symmetric bilinear form \(B\), a handful of Gram determinants, and a field \(\mathbb{F}\) with \(\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F}) \neq 2\).

That framework — quadrance for the “squared distance” between points, spread for the “squared angle” between lines, and quadrea for the invariant of a whole triangle — is the backbone of Wildberger’s Universal Hyperbolic Geometry. It works because rational trigonometry refuses to privilege the real numbers or the transcendental functions built on them; it insists that everything metrical reduces to polynomial identities that make sense over any field of characteristic not two.

The natural question is: what happens one dimension up? If a projective triangle in \(\mathbb{P}^2\) has a quadrea, what does a projective tetrahedron in \(\mathbb{P}^3\) have? John D. Coady’s paper, On the Projective Quadrume and the Recursive Factorization of Projective Simplices, answers this directly. It defines the projective quadrume of a tetrahedron as a normalized \(4\times4\) Gram determinant, proves that it factorizes cleanly into a face quadrea times an altitude quadrance, and then shows that this is just the first non-trivial case of a completely general recursive theorem for simplices of any dimension. This post walks through that construction.

The Algebraic Setting

Everything rests on a single piece of data: an \(n\)-dimensional vector space \(\mathbb{V}^n\) over a field \(\mathbb{F}\) with \(\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F}) \neq 2\), equipped with a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form \(B(u,v)\). The characteristic restriction is not a technicality to wave away — it is what keeps a symmetric bilinear form from collapsing into an alternating one, and it keeps the number \(2\) invertible, which the definitions below lean on constantly.

Quadrance. For a vector \(v \in \mathbb{V}^n\), \[ Q(v) \equiv B(v,v). \] A vector is null if \(Q(v) = 0\); the theory only tracks non-null representatives.

Projective points and projective quadrance. A point \(a_i\) of \(\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\) is a one-dimensional subspace of \(\mathbb{V}^n\), represented by a non-null vector \(v_i\). The projective quadrance between two points is \[ q(a_1,a_2) \equiv 1 - \frac{B(v_1,v_2)^2}{Q(v_1)\,Q(v_2)}. \]

The projective Gram volume. This is the key generalization. Given \(k\) vectors \(v_1,\dots,v_k\), form the \(k\times k\) Gram matrix \([G_k]_{ij} = B(v_i,v_j)\). The projective Gram volume is the normalized determinant \[ \mathcal{Q}_k \equiv \frac{\det(G_k)}{\prod_{i=1}^{k} Q(v_i)}. \]

This single quantity unifies the whole hierarchy: at \(k=2\) it is the projective quadrance, at \(k=3\) it is Wildberger’s projective quadrea \(\mathcal{S}\), and at \(k=4\) it is what Coady names the projective quadrume \(\mathcal{V}\) — the natural continuation of Wildberger’s naming scheme for the tetrahedral case.

Crucially, \(\mathcal{Q}_k\) doesn’t care which representative vectors you chose. Scaling \(v_i \to \lambda_i v_i\) multiplies \(\det(G_k)\) by \(\prod \lambda_i^2\) and multiplies \(\prod Q(v_i)\) by the same factor, so the ratio is invariant — exactly the projective invariance you need for this to be a well-defined geometric quantity rather than an artifact of coordinates.

Why Gram Determinants Factor: The Exterior Algebra Picture

Before stating the tetrahedral theorem, it’s worth seeing why Gram determinants factor at all, because the reason is geometric, not just algebraic bookkeeping.

The Gram determinant of \(k\) vectors has a coordinate-free meaning in the exterior algebra \(\bigwedge^k \mathbb{V}^n\): \[ \det(G_k) = \langle v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k,\; v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k \rangle. \] If you decompose the last vector \(v_k = v_k^{\parallel} + v_k^{\perp}\) relative to the span of the first \(k-1\) vectors, the parallel component contributes nothing to the wedge product — a wedge with a repeated direction vanishes — so \[ v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_{k-1} \wedge v_k = v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_{k-1} \wedge v_k^{\perp}. \] This is the exterior-algebra shadow of the familiar Euclidean fact that (squared base) \(\times\) (squared height) = squared volume. Made precise as a Gram determinant statement:

Lemma (Gram Determinant Factorization). If \(U_{k-1} \subset U_k\) with \(\dim U_{k-1} = k-1\), \(\dim U_k = k\), and \(B|_{U_{k-1}}\) is non-degenerate, then \[\det(G_k) = \det(G_{k-1}) \cdot Q(v_k^{\perp}),\] where \(v_k^{\perp}\) is the component of \(v_k\) orthogonal to \(U_{k-1}\).

The proof is a congruence transformation: replace \(v_k\) by \(v_k^{\perp}\) using an upper-triangular change of basis of determinant \(1\), which block-diagonalizes the Gram matrix without changing its determinant. Everything downstream in the paper is this lemma, applied at increasing dimension and translated into projective language.

The Dual Point of a Plane

To turn “orthogonal complement of a \(3\)-dimensional subspace” into something computable, Coady gives an explicit cofactor construction. If \(v_1,v_2,v_3 \in \mathbb{V}^4\) span a non-degenerate subspace \(U\), form the \(3\times4\) matrix \(M\) whose rows are \(v_1^T G\), \(v_2^T G\), \(v_3^T G\) (here \(G\) is the matrix of \(B\) in coordinates). The dual point \(n\) orthogonal to \(U\) is given, up to scale, by the signed \(3\times3\) cofactors of \(M\): \[ n_i \equiv (-1)^i \det(\hat M_i), \] where \(\hat M_i\) deletes the \(i\)-th column. A Laplace-expansion argument shows this automatically satisfies \(B(v_i, n) = 0\) for \(i=1,2,3\), and non-degeneracy of \(B\) on the ambient space plus non-degeneracy of \(B|_U\) together force \(Q(n) \neq 0\) — the dual point is itself non-null, so it represents a genuine projective point.

This is the algebraic stand-in for “the point at infinity in the direction perpendicular to a plane” — except there is no metric distance being invoked anywhere, only the bilinear form.

The Polynomial Gram Determinant Identity

With the dual point in hand, Coady states the central algebraic result of the tetrahedral case.

Theorem (Polynomial Gram Determinant Identity). Let \(v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4 \in \mathbb{V}^4\) with \(U = \mathrm{span}(v_1,v_2,v_3)\) non-degenerate, and let \(n\) be the cofactor dual of \(v_1,v_2,v_3\). Then, identically as polynomials in the coordinates, \[ \det(G_4)\, Q(n) = \det(G_3)\, B(v_4, n)^2. \]

The proof combines the two previous ingredients. The Gram Determinant Factorization Lemma gives \(\det(G_4) = \det(G_3) \cdot Q(v_4^{\perp})\), and the orthogonal projection of \(v_4\) onto the line spanned by \(n\) is \(v_4^{\perp} = \frac{B(v_4,n)}{Q(n)} n\), so \[ Q(v_4^{\perp}) = \frac{B(v_4,n)^2}{Q(n)}. \] Substituting and clearing the denominator \(Q(n)\) produces the identity. The subtlety Coady is careful about is that this argument, on its face, only works where \(B|_U\) is non-degenerate. But since both sides of the identity are polynomials in the coordinates of \(v_1,\dots,v_4\) (the cofactor entries of \(n\) are themselves polynomial), and they agree on a Zariski-dense open set, they must agree everywhere — including at degenerate configurations where the geometric argument alone would not apply.

The Quadrume Factorization Theorem

Dividing the Polynomial Gram Determinant Identity through by \(Q(v_1)Q(v_2)Q(v_3)Q(v_4)Q(n)\) turns a statement about raw determinants into a statement about projective invariants. Define the projective altitude quadrance from vertex \(a_4\) to the opposite face \(a_1a_2a_3\) as \[ h(a_4) \equiv \frac{B(v_4,n)^2}{Q(v_4)\,Q(n)} = 1 - q(a_4, n), \] the projective quadrance between \(a_4\) and the dual point \(n\) of its opposite face. Then:

Quadrume Factorization Theorem. For four non-coplanar points \(a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4 \in \mathbb{P}^3\) with non-degenerate face span, \[ \boxed{\mathcal{V} = \mathcal{S} \times h(a_4)} \] where \(\mathcal{V}\) is the projective quadrume of the tetrahedron and \(\mathcal{S}\) is the projective quadrea of the opposite face.

This is exactly the algebraic tetrahedral analogue of *(base area)\(^2\) \(\times\) (height)\(^2\) = (volume)\(^2\), up to the appropriate normalization — except stated with quadrances and quadreas instead of lengths and areas, and valid over any field of characteristic not two, in any signature.

A companion result records how the identity behaves under rescaling representative vectors: the unnormalized polynomial identity picks up a factor of \(\prod_i \lambda_i^2 \cdot \lambda_n^2\) on both sides, while the normalized quadrume factorization is completely scale-invariant, as it must be for a genuinely projective statement.

The General \(n\)-Dimensional Theorem

The tetrahedral case turns out to be nothing special — it’s the \(k=4\) instance of a fully general recursion. Define the altitude quadrance of the \(k\)-th vertex relative to the span of the preceding \(k-1\) points in exactly the same way, using the dual normal \(n\) of the codimension-one face \(U_{k-1} \subset U_k\): \[ h(a_k) \equiv \frac{B(v_k,n)^2}{Q(v_k)\,Q(n)}. \]

\(n\)-Dimensional Factorization Theorem. Let \(a_1,\dots,a_k\) be linearly independent points in \(\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\) (\(k \le n\)) with \(B|_{U_{k-1}}\) non-degenerate. Then \[ \mathcal{Q}_k = \mathcal{Q}_{k-1} \times h(a_k). \]

The proof is the identical two-step argument — Gram factorization lemma, then normalize — just carried out at general \(k\) instead of \(k=4\). Applied recursively down a flag of non-degenerate subspaces \(U_1 \subset U_2 \subset \cdots \subset U_{k-1}\), it telescopes into a full chain factorization \[ \mathcal{Q}_k = \prod_{j=2}^{k} h(a_j), \] expressing the hypervolume invariant of an entire simplex as a product of successive altitude quadrances — the projective, algebraic analogue of building up a simplex’s volume one dimension at a time.

Two caveats are worth keeping in mind. First, degeneracy: if any representative vector is null, or if \(\det(G_{k-1}) = 0\) (either because the first \(k-1\) points are linearly dependent, or because \(B\) restricted to their span is itself degenerate), the normalized invariant \(\mathcal{Q}_k\) simply isn’t defined, even though the underlying polynomial identity still holds. Second, sign: because \(\mathcal{Q}_k\) behaves as a squared hypervolume, it can be negative in indefinite signatures such as the Lorentzian \((+,+,+,-)\) — consistent with how Wildberger’s quadrance and quadrea already handle indefinite geometries without resorting to absolute values. In the positive-definite Euclidean limit, \(\sqrt{|\mathcal{Q}_k|}\) recovers (up to a fixed scaling convention) the classical Cayley–Menger volume of the simplex.

Independent Symbolic Verification

A clean way to build confidence in an identity like this — beyond the synthetic proof — is to throw a computer algebra system at it directly, with a fully general symbolic metric. Coady’s appendix does exactly that: it builds four symbolic projective points, a general diagonal symbolic bilinear form (any symmetric bilinear form can be diagonalized over a field of characteristic \(\neq 2\)), constructs the dual point via the cofactor formula, and checks that

\[ \Delta \equiv \det(G_4)\,Q(n) - \det(G_3)\,B(v_4,n)^2 \]

simplifies identically to zero.

import sympy as sp

def run_symbolic_proof():
    # 1. Symbolic coordinates for 4 general projective points
    A = list(sp.symbols('a0:4'))
    B_pt = list(sp.symbols('b0:4'))
    C = list(sp.symbols('c0:4'))
    D = list(sp.symbols('d0:4'))

    # 2. General diagonal symbolic metric (WLOG, char(F) != 2)
    g0, g1, g2, g3 = sp.symbols('g0 g1 g2 g3')
    G = sp.diag(g0, g1, g2, g3)

    def B_form(u, v):
        u_mat, v_mat = sp.Matrix(u), sp.Matrix(v)
        return (u_mat.T * G * v_mat)[0, 0]

    # 3. Cofactor construction of the dual point n orthogonal to span(A,B,C)
    def symbolic_dual_point(A, B_pt, C):
        r1 = list(sp.Matrix(A).T * G)
        r2 = list(sp.Matrix(B_pt).T * G)
        r3 = list(sp.Matrix(C).T * G)
        M = sp.Matrix([r1, r2, r3])
        n = []
        for i in range(4):
            M_sub = M.copy()
            M_sub.col_del(i)
            n.append((-1)**i * M_sub.det())
        return n

    # 4. Gram matrices G4, G3
    pts = [A, B_pt, C, D]
    G4 = sp.Matrix(4, 4, lambda i, j: B_form(pts[i], pts[j]))
    G3 = sp.Matrix(3, 3, lambda i, j: B_form(pts[i], pts[j]))

    det_G4, det_G3 = G4.det(), G3.det()
    n = symbolic_dual_point(A, B_pt, C)

    Q_n = B_form(n, n)
    B_D_n = B_form(D, n)

    LHS = det_G4 * Q_n
    RHS = det_G3 * (B_D_n**2)

    difference = sp.simplify(LHS - RHS)
    print("Symbolic Difference (LHS - RHS) =", difference)
    print("Is the identity proved?", difference == 0)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    run_symbolic_proof()

Running this (it takes roughly a minute or two, since simplify is working over sixteen free coordinate variables and four free metric parameters) prints Symbolic Difference = 0 and Is the identity proved? True. This is a genuinely independent check: the symbolic computation never assumes the synthetic proof’s structure, it just multiplies out sixteen coordinates against an arbitrary diagonal metric and confirms the two sides collapse to the same polynomial.

Closing Thoughts

The projective quadrume is a satisfying instance of a pattern that shows up whenever rational trigonometry is extended: define the natural normalized Gram invariant, prove a factorization lemma using nothing but bilinear-form algebra and a change of basis, and watch the identity survive intact at every dimension. What starts as “the quadrea of a triangle” becomes “the quadrume of a tetrahedron” becomes, ultimately, a single recursive law \[ \mathcal{Q}_k = \mathcal{Q}_{k-1} \times h(a_k) \] governing the projective Gram volume of a simplex of any size in a space of any dimension, over a field of any characteristic other than two. No transcendental functions were used in the making of this theorem — and that, per Wildberger’s original philosophy, is precisely the point.
