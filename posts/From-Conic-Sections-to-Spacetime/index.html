The two topics are connected by analogy; I say below where the analogy is exact and where it is only suggestive.

1. Two stories with a shared ingredient

Story A (classical geometry). Ellipses, hyperbolas and parabolas look very different over the reals, yet in the complex projective plane they are projectively equivalent.

Story B (discrete spacetime). The group \(SO(1,3)\) of Lorentz transformations can be described exactly, over a finite field \(\mathbb{F}_p\) with \(p \equiv 3 \pmod 4\), using \(2\times 2\) matrices with entries in the quadratic extension \(\mathbb{F}_p[i]\).

The shared ingredient is simple: adjoining \(\sqrt{-1}\) makes a sum of two squares factor as a product, \(x^2 + y^2 = (x+iy)(x-iy)\), and homogenizing puts all the resulting quadrics on equal footing. These are closely parallel moves, not the same machinery. The rest of this post tracks the parallel, and flags where it stops.

2. The conic story

The real trichotomy

Start with the general conic \(ax^2 + bxy + cy^2 + dx + ey + f = 0\) with \((a,b,c) \neq (0,0,0)\). The quadratic part controls the shape, and the linear terms only move the curve around. Write the quadratic part as

\[ \begin{pmatrix} x & y \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a & b/2 \\ b/2 & c \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det A = ac - \tfrac{b^2}{4} = -\tfrac14\,(b^2 - 4ac). \]

By the spectral theorem, a real symmetric matrix is diagonalized by an orthogonal change of basis. For the video’s example \(5x^2 + 4xy + 5y^2 = 9\), the eigenvalues are 3 and 7, the rotation is by \(45^\circ\), and the curve becomes \(7u^2 + 3v^2 = 9\), i.e. \(\frac{u^2}{9/7} + \frac{v^2}{3} = 1\).

Conic \(\det A\) \(b^2 - 4ac\) Topology over \(\mathbb{R}\)
Ellipse \(>0\) \(<0\) compact, 1 component
Hyperbola \(<0\) \(>0\) unbounded, 2 branches
Parabola \(=0\) \(=0\) unbounded in one direction, 1 component

Step 1: complexify

Over \(\mathbb{C}\), substituting \(y \mapsto iy\) turns \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\) into \(x^2 - y^2 = 1\): ellipse and hyperbola merge. With \(u = x+iy\) and \(v = x-iy\) the curve is \(uv = 1\), which is the punctured plane \(\mathbb{C}^\times\). The parabola \(y = x^2\) is parametrized by \(x \in \mathbb{C}\), so it is all of \(\mathbb{C}\). Since \(\mathbb{C}\) and \(\mathbb{C}^\times\) are not homeomorphic, the parabola is still different.

Step 2: projectivize

Pass to \(\mathbb{CP}^2\) with coordinates \([x:y:z]\) and homogenize:

  • ellipse: \(x^2 + y^2 = z^2\)
  • parabola: \(yz = x^2\)

Rearranging, \(x^2 = (z-y)(z+y)\). Setting \(X = x\), \(Y = z - y\), \(Z = z + y\) gives \(X^2 = YZ\). So the two conics are related by a linear change of projective coordinates. The ellipse and hyperbola (\(\mathbb{C}^\times\)) pick up two points at infinity, the parabola (\(\mathbb{C}\)) picks up one, and all three become a copy of \(\mathbb{CP}^1\), the Riemann sphere. More precisely: every non-degenerate conic in \(\mathbb{CP}^2\) is projectively equivalent to every other.

A finite-field echo

The same dichotomy shows up over \(\mathbb{F}_p\) with \(p \equiv 3 \pmod 4\), where \(-1\) is a non-square. The curve \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\) has \(p+1\) points and \(x^2 - y^2 = 1\) has \(p - 1\) points (check \(p = 3\): 4 points and 2 points). They are genuinely different over \(\mathbb{F}_p\), and become equivalent after adjoining \(i\), which is exactly the situation in Story B. This is a standard point-count, not something from the paper.

3. The spacetime story

My preprint builds an exact model of \((1+3)\)-dimensional Lorentz geometry over \(\mathbb{F}_p\), in the spirit of N. J. Wildberger’s rational trigonometry: no angles, no rapidities, no transcendental functions, only field operations.

The Witt-index obstruction, and what it does and doesn’t say

The form \(x_0^2 - x_1^2 - x_2^2 - x_3^2\) over \(\mathbb{F}_p\) (\(p \equiv 3 \pmod 4\)) has Witt index 1 (“elliptic”). The determinant on \(M_2(\mathbb{F}_p)\) has Witt index 2 (“split”). So there is no linear isometry between the Lorentz form and \((M_2(\mathbb{F}_p), \det)\). An earlier paper in this series proved that and built a \(4\times 4\) Clifford-algebra engine instead.

The obstruction only excludes embeddings into \(M_2(\mathbb{F}_p)\). It says nothing about a different quadratic space. Take \(K = \mathbb{F}_p[i] \cong \mathbb{F}_{p^2}\) and the four-dimensional \(\mathbb{F}_p\)-space of Hermitian matrices in \(M_2(K)\):

\[ X(x) = \begin{pmatrix} x_0 + x_3 & x_1 - i x_2 \\ x_1 + i x_2 & x_0 - x_3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det X(x) = x_0^2 - x_1^2 - x_2^2 - x_3^2 . \]

This is a hyperbolic plane plus an anisotropic plane, so it has Witt index 1 and discriminant \(-1\), matching the Lorentz form. Nothing is “bypassed” and no theorem is violated; the paper simply uses a different quadratic space than the one the obstruction excludes. (The earlier paper even noted that extending scalars was not ruled out.)

Rotors and the rotor quadric

A general element of \(M_2(K)\) is \(G = xI + \mathbf{z}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\) with \(x = a + ip\) and \(\mathbf{z} = \boldsymbol{\kappa} + i\boldsymbol{\rho}\). Its determinant is

\[ \det G = (a^2 - p^2 - \boldsymbol{\kappa}^2 + \boldsymbol{\rho}^2) + 2i\,(ap - \boldsymbol{\kappa}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}). \]

It is \(\mathbb{F}_p\)-valued exactly when \(ap = \boldsymbol{\kappa}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}\). That equation defines the rotor quadric in \(\mathbb{P}^7(\mathbb{F}_p)\). Each non-null rotor acts on Hermitian matrices by \(X \mapsto G X G^\dagger / N\) with \(N = a^2 - p^2 - \boldsymbol{\kappa}^2 + \boldsymbol{\rho}^2\), and this gives an isomorphism with \(SO(1,3;\mathbb{F}_p)\). (The isomorphism is proved in the companion paper on rational rotations of four-dimensional relativistic space.)

How close is the analogy with \(X^2 = YZ\)? Both are split quadrics given by a product-form equation, and in both cases passing to projective coordinates lets everything be treated uniformly. But they are not the same object: \(YZ = X^2\) is a rank-3 conic in \(\mathbb{P}^2\) obtained by factoring a difference of squares, while \(ap = \boldsymbol{\kappa}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}\) is a rank-8 split quadric in \(\mathbb{P}^7\) obtained by requiring an imaginary part to vanish. Calling one a “generalization” of the other would be too strong.

Points at infinity: the half-turns

The affine description of a rotation by its “anchor” \(\boldsymbol{\zeta} = \mathbf{z}/x\) breaks down when \(x = a + ip = 0\). In the rotor picture these are simply the points with \(a = p = 0\) (and so \(\boldsymbol{\kappa}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho} = 0\)), which are the half-turns. They are ordinary points of the rotor quadric; the projective description does not “add” them, it just makes them visible as part of the group. Over \(\mathbb{F}_7\) there are exactly \(p^4 + p^2 = 2{,}450\) of them, out of \(|SO(1,3;\mathbb{F}_7)| = 117{,}600\). Both numbers were confirmed by direct enumeration.

5. What the preprint establishes

All numerical claims below come from one script, verify_rotor_model.py (exact finite-field arithmetic, no floating-point tolerances in the algebra), whose output is reproduced in the Zenodo record.

Anchor recovery. For a Lorentz transformation \(\Lambda\) with \(\operatorname{tr}\Lambda \neq 0\), the six-component anchor is \[ A(\Lambda) = \frac{2\Lambda_{\mathrm{as}}}{\operatorname{tr}\Lambda}, \qquad \Lambda_{\mathrm{as}} = \tfrac12\left(\Lambda - \eta\Lambda^T\eta\right), \] with no matrix inversion. This is a chart statement: it recovers the unique anchor whose cap is \(\Lambda\). If your element is already a \(2\times 2\) spinor \(xI + \mathbf{z}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\), the anchor is just \(\mathbf{z}/x\).

Not every anchor has a rational cap. A cap exists over \(\mathbb{F}_p\) only if \((1-\alpha)^2 + \beta^2\) is a non-zero square, where \(\zeta\cdot\zeta = \alpha + i\beta\). Over \(\mathbb{F}_7\), among all \(7^6 = 117{,}649\) anchors, 57,575 admit a rational cap, 57,624 do not, and 2,450 lie on the excluded locus \(\zeta\cdot\zeta = 1\). So the anchor charts still have genuine exceptional sets. What is total is the projective rotor product.

Anchor doubling. The spinor Cayley map \(\mathrm{ca}(\zeta) = (I+\zeta\cdot\sigma)(I-\zeta\cdot\sigma)^{-1} \in SL(2,K)\) induces, by conjugation, a Lorentz rotation whose anchor is \[ \zeta' = \frac{2\zeta}{1 + \zeta\cdot\zeta}, \] so the spin chart and the vector chart differ by a nonlinear doubling, as in \(SU(2) \to SO(3)\).

The 11% anomaly, stated carefully. An earlier preprint composed \((1+2)\)-dimensional edge anchors with Wildberger’s anchor product and found the predicted trace matched the real \(2\times 2\) computation only about 11% of the time. The corrected comparison uses doubled anchors and the sign-free quantity \(T^2\), which in \((1+2)\) dimensions satisfies \(T^2 = 1 - p\) with \(p\) the half spread. (The \((1+3)\) analogues are \(w^2 = 1 - \pi\) and \(N_{K/\mathbb{F}_p}(w) = 1 - s\).) Over primes 7, 11, 13 and 17, all 1.7 million valid test pairs agree exactly. By contrast, the older target \((1-2p)^2\) agrees only 70%, 38%, 35% and 24% of the time. This corrects a failure mode of the same type and explains why it fails; it does not reproduce the original 11% figure, which came from a different script. The test is in \((1+2)\) dimensions.

Edge transports and holonomy. For non-null vertices \(u, v\), the preprint defines an edge transport \(U_{uv} = G_{uv}^2/\det G_{uv}\) with \(G_{uv} = X(u)X(v)^\vee\). The square is a modeling choice (an ansatz): the unsquared product around a closed loop telescopes to a central scalar, i.e. a flat connection. The squared transport rotates by twice the reflection-pair angle and is not a transport carrying \([u]\) to \([v]\), so it should not be read as a derivation of curvature. Over \(\mathbb{F}_7\) (350 non-null points, 68,600 admissible ordered edges), exhaustively enumerating ordered admissible 4-cycles with the first vertex fixed to one representative per square class gives 4,597,908 cycles per class; 99.757% have non-trivial holonomy. The remaining 0.243% may be coplanar configurations: in a random sample of 9,097 non-coplanar cycles, none was trivial. That is evidence, not a proof; an exhaustive non-coplanar count is left for later work.

Ghost parity, as an analogy. The \(\mathbb{Z}_2\) grading \(\varepsilon = \prod_{i=1}^4 \chi_p(Q(v_i))\) on a tetrahedron’s four vertices is the spinor-norm character of an open reflection chain, and it is tied to the projective quadrume by \(\chi_p(\mathcal{V}) = -\varepsilon\). This is an algebraic analogue of the ghost-parity grading that Bateman and Turok use in higher-derivative quantum field theory. It is close to a definition, and sector decoupling in the dynamics holds by construction through the mutation rule. In their setting the corresponding statement is a nontrivial theorem about an interacting theory, which this model does not reproduce.

6. What this does and does not claim

It gives: a compact \(2\times 2\) description of the finite-field Lorentz group, exact counts that were checked by enumeration, a clear account of why one numerical comparison failed, and a reproducible script.

It does not give: curvature from holonomy, a justification of the squared transport beyond calling it an ansatz, the Bateman–Turok positivity result (finite fields have no ordering, so “positive” is meaningless here), or any continuum limit. The relation between the projective quadrume and Regge-type curvature, and the large-\(p\) limit, are listed as open problems.

7. Try it

Download the Zenodo record at doi:10.5281/zenodo.23138782 and run

pip install numpy
python verify_rotor_model.py

Expect 100.00% agreement in Part 1 at every prime, 99.757% non-trivial holonomy in Part 2, and the anchor counts 57,575 / 57,624 / 2,450 in Part 3. The random-sample figures depend on NumPy’s generator, so digits in those lines may shift across major NumPy versions.
