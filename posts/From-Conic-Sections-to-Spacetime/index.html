1. A Tale of Two Unifications

At first glance, few topics seem farther apart than high-school conic sections and the quantum geometry of relativistic spacetime:

  1. Problem A (Classical Geometry): Why do ellipses, hyperbolas, and parabolas look drastically different in Euclidean space, yet modern algebraic geometers claim they are the exact same curve?
  2. Problem B (Quantum Gravity & Spacetime): Why can \((1+3)\)-dimensional Lorentzian spacetime and the group \(\mathrm{SO}(1,3)\) be governed by \(2 \times 2\) matrices over a finite field without transcendental functions, matrix exponentials, or singular charts?

Recently, a popular lecture walked viewers through the algebraic machinery that unifies conics: writing quadratic forms as symmetric \(2 \times 2\) matrices, extending coordinates from \(\mathbb{R}\) to \(\mathbb{C}\), and adding “points at infinity” to build the complex projective plane \(\mathbb{CP}^2\).

Simultaneously, John D. Coady’s research paper, A Projective \(2 \times 2\) Rotor Architecture for \((1+3)\)D Discrete Spacetime: Holonomies, Ghost Parity, and the Double-Cover Anchor Map, addresses the architecture of discrete spacetime.

Remarkably, both works rely on the exact same algebraic engine: \[\text{Affine Quadratic Forms} \xrightarrow{\quad\text{Complexify / Field Extension}\quad} \text{Split Geometries} \xrightarrow{\quad\text{Projectivize}\quad} \text{Singularity-Free Space}\]

Let’s explore how the geometric tricks used to turn an ellipse into a parabola provide the exact mathematical blueprint for computing spacetime holonomies in \(2 \times 2\) matrices.

2. The 2D Blueprint: The Secret Life of Conics

In the video, the presenter begins with the general second-degree Cartesian equation: \[ax^2 + bxy + cy^2 + dx + ey + f = 0, \quad (a, b, c) \neq (0, 0, 0)\]

The degree-two terms control the geometry, while the linear terms simply move the curve around. Writing the homogeneous quadratic part as a vector-matrix-vector product gives:

\[\begin{pmatrix} x & y \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a & b/2 \\ b/2 & c \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}, \quad A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b/2 \\ b/2 & c \end{pmatrix}\]

The determinant of \(A\) connects directly to the high-school discriminant: \[\det(A) = ac - \frac{b^2}{4} = -\frac{1}{4}(b^2 - 4ac)\]

By the Spectral Theorem, any real symmetric matrix can be diagonalized by an orthogonal rotation matrix \(P\). In the video’s worked example, \(5x^2 + 4xy + 5y^2 = 9\) is rotated by \(45^\circ\) (\(\pi/4\)) into the canonical unrotated ellipse \(\frac{u^2}{9/7} + \frac{v^2}{3} = 1\).

The Real Trichotomy (\(\mathbb{R}^2\))

Over the real numbers, conics are locked into three rigid geometric and topological compartments:

Conic Determinant Discriminant Topology
Ellipse \(\det A > 0\) \(b^2 - 4ac < 0\) Compact, closed, 1 connected component
Hyperbola \(\det A < 0\) \(b^2 - 4ac > 0\) Unbounded (2 directions), 2 disconnected branches
Parabola \(\det A = 0\) \(b^2 - 4ac = 0\) Unbounded (1 direction), 1 connected component

Step 1: Complexification (\(\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{C}^2\))

What happens if \((x, y) \in \mathbb{C}^2\)? Consider the unit circle/ellipse: \[x^2 + y^2 = 1\]

Substitute \(y \mapsto iy\). The equation becomes: \[x^2 + (iy)^2 = 1 \implies x^2 - y^2 = 1\]

In an instant, the ellipse becomes a hyperbola. Over \(\mathbb{C}\), the signature distinction between positive-definite (\(++\)) and indefinite (\(+-\)) evaporates:

  • Factoring \(x^2 + y^2 = (x+iy)(x-iy) = 1\) with \(u = x+iy\) and \(v = x-iy\) gives \(uv = 1 \implies v = 1/u\).
  • Topologically, this is the punctured complex plane \(\mathbb{C}^\times = \mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}\).
  • But what about the parabola \(y = x^2\)? That curve is parametrized by \(x \in \mathbb{C}\), meaning it is homeomorphic to the full complex plane \(\mathbb{C}\).

Over \(\mathbb{C}^2\), the ellipse and hyperbola have merged, but the parabola remains distinct because \(\mathbb{C} \not\cong \mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}\).

Step 2: Projectivization (\(\mathbb{C}^2 \to \mathbb{CP}^2\))

To bridge the final gap, we introduce projective coordinates \([x : y : z] \in \mathbb{CP}^2\), where \([x : y : z] = [\lambda x : \lambda y : \lambda z]\) for \(\lambda \neq 0\): * Affine Chart (\(z \neq 0\)): Dividing by \(z\) yields \([x/z : y/z : 1] = [X : Y : 1] \cong \mathbb{C}^2\). * Line at Infinity (\(z = 0\)): The directions \([x : y : 0]\) form the line \(\ell_\infty \cong \mathbb{CP}^1\).

Now, homogenize the equations by inserting \(z\): * Projective Ellipse: \(x^2 + y^2 = z^2\) * Projective Parabola: \(yz = x^2\)

Can we transform one into the other? Rearrange the ellipse: \[x^2 = z^2 - y^2 = (z - y)(z + y)\]

Define a linear change of projective coordinates: \[X = x, \quad Y = z - y, \quad Z = z + y\]

Under this linear substitution, the equation becomes: \[X^2 = YZ\]

The Geometric Punchline

In \(\mathbb{CP}^2\), every non-degenerate conic is identical. Adding the line at infinity fills the “puncture” in \(\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}\), compactifying both curves into a projective line \(\mathbb{CP}^1\) (the Riemann sphere).

3. The 4D Engine: Discrete Spacetime in \(2 \times 2\) Matrices

Now consider the physics side. In Coady’s paper, the goal is to build a discrete, exact model of \((1+3)\mathrm{D}\) relativistic spacetime over finite fields \(\mathbb{F}_p\) (\(p \equiv 3 \pmod 4\)), inspired by N. J. Wildberger’s rational trigonometry.

The moment you attempt this in 4D, you run into three major roadblocks: 1. The Curse of Dimension: Standard Lorentz rotations in \(4 \times 4\) matrices involve cubic/quartic Cayley inversions and transcendental functions (\(\cosh, \sinh, \exp\)). 2. The Witt-Index Obstruction: The Lorentzian metric on \(\mathbb{F}_p^4\) has signature \((+ - - -)\) and Witt index 1 (elliptic type). But \(M_2(\mathbb{F}_p)\) with the determinant has signature \((++ - -)\) and Witt index 2 (split type). Witt’s theorem strictly forbids any linear isometry between the two! 3. The Affine Anchor Breakdown: Composing rotations via affine Lie algebra generators (anchors) fails at half-turns and composition denominators (\(1 - \langle u, v \rangle = 0\)).

How does Coady solve all three? By following the exact same playbook as the conic video.

The Dimension–Degree Coincidence

Why \(2 \times 2\) matrices? In Appendix A of the paper, Coady highlights a remarkable fact:

\[\text{In } M_n(F), \quad \deg(\det M) = n\]

  • For \(n = 3\), the determinant is cubic (volume).
  • For \(n = 4\), the determinant is quartic (hyper-volume).
  • Only for \(n = 2\) does the determinant polynomial have degree 2—matching metric quadrance!

Because \(2 \times 2\) determinants are quadratic forms, matrix multiplicativity \(\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)\) automatically gives metric isometries for free via the rotor sandwich: \[X \mapsto \frac{GXG^\dagger}{\det G}\]

Step 1: The Galois Field Extension (\(y \mapsto iy\) in Spacetime)

Just as the video substituted \(y \mapsto iy\) to turn an ellipse into a hyperbola, Coady overcomes the Witt-index obstruction by extending \(\mathbb{F}_p\) to the quadratic Galois extension: \[K = \mathbb{F}_p[i] = \mathbb{F}_p[T]/(T^2 + 1) \cong \mathbb{F}_{p^2}\]

Instead of looking at all of \(M_2(\mathbb{F}_p)\), he restricts to the \(\mathbb{F}_p\)-vector space of Hermitian matrices \(\mathrm{Herm}_{\mathbb{F}_p} \subset M_2(K)\): \[X(x) = x_0 I_2 + \mathbf{x} \cdot \boldsymbol{\sigma} = \begin{pmatrix} x_0 + x_3 & x_1 - ix_2 \\ x_1 + ix_2 & x_0 - x_3 \end{pmatrix}\]

Taking the determinant: \[\det X(x) = (x_0 + x_3)(x_0 - x_3) - (x_1 - ix_2)(x_1 + ix_2) = x_0^2 - x_1^2 - x_2^2 - x_3^2 = Q(x)\]

The field determinant over \(K\) natively reproduces the \((1,3)\) Minkowski metric, solving the Witt-index obstruction!

Step 2: The Projective Rotor Quadric (\(X^2 = YZ\) in 8 Dimensions)

Next, how do we parametrize Lorentz transformations without transcendentals or division-by-zero singularities?

In the video, the projective conic in split coordinates was: \[X^2 = YZ \implies YZ - X^2 = 0\]

In Coady’s paper, a general rotor in \(M_2(K)\) is written as \(G_R = xI_2 + \mathbf{z} \cdot \boldsymbol{\sigma}\), where \(x = a + ip\) and \(\mathbf{z} = \boldsymbol{\kappa} + i\boldsymbol{\rho}\). Computing its determinant gives: \[\det G_R = (a^2 - p^2 - \boldsymbol{\kappa}^2 + \boldsymbol{\rho}^2) + 2i(ap - \boldsymbol{\kappa} \cdot \boldsymbol{\rho})\]

To guarantee that \(G_R\) induces an \(\mathbb{F}_p\)-valued Lorentz transformation, \(\det G_R\) must lie in \(\mathbb{F}_p\), meaning the imaginary part must vanish: \[ap = \boldsymbol{\kappa} \cdot \boldsymbol{\rho}\]

This defines the projective rotor quadric \(\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{F}_p) \subset \mathbb{P}^7(\mathbb{F}_p)\): \[\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{F}_p) \equiv \left\{ [a : \boldsymbol{\kappa} : \boldsymbol{\rho} : p] \in \mathbb{P}^7(\mathbb{F}_p) : ap = \boldsymbol{\kappa} \cdot \boldsymbol{\rho} \right\}\]

Look closely at \(ap = \boldsymbol{\kappa} \cdot \boldsymbol{\rho}\). It is the exact 8-dimensional generalization of the projective parabola \(YZ = X^2\)! By projectivizing into \(\mathbb{P}^7\), the space of rotors is closed under polynomial multiplication.

The troublesome half-turns (where affine charts blow up) are simply the points at infinity where \(a = p = 0\): \[[0 : \boldsymbol{\kappa} : \boldsymbol{\rho} : 0] \quad \text{with} \quad \boldsymbol{\kappa} \cdot \boldsymbol{\rho} = 0\]

5. The Physical Payoff: Why This Matters for Quantum Gravity

Why go through all this algebraic geometry? In discrete spacetime models (such as Regge calculus or causal dynamical triangulations), computing curvature around closed simplicial loops (Wilson loops) traditionally requires composing rotation matrices using angles, rapidities, and trigonometric functions:

  1. Floating-point errors accumulate: Every \(\cos(\theta)\) and \(\operatorname{artanh}(\beta)\) evaluation introduces numerical drift that violates metric conservation.
  2. Infinite index over \(\mathbb{Q}\) and \(\mathbb{F}_p\): Wildberger showed that classical unit quaternions miss an infinite subgroup of rational rotations. Projective proportions capture 100% of them.
  3. The Anchor-Doubling Law: In Version 4 of Coady’s framework, composing anchors directly using Wildberger’s formula yielded an empirical match of only \(\approx 11\%\). Recognizing that the spin-cover \(\mathrm{SL}(2, K)\) and the vector group \(\mathrm{SO}(1,3)\) live in two projectively related charts: \[\boldsymbol{\zeta}' = \frac{2\boldsymbol{\zeta}}{1 + \boldsymbol{\zeta} \cdot \boldsymbol{\zeta}}, \quad T^2 = 1 - p\] resolved the anomaly completely, producing 100.00% exact agreement across \(1.7 \times 10^6\) test pairs.
  4. Ghost Parity and Quadrume: In discrete 4-simplices (tetrahedra), Coady shows that the open reflection product \(R_\gamma = X(v_1)X(v_2)^\vee X(v_3)X(v_4)^\vee\) has determinant: \[\det(R_\gamma) = \prod_{i=1}^4 Q(v_i) \equiv N_\gamma\] Its spinor norm character \(\chi_p(N_\gamma) = \varepsilon(\gamma)\) couples directly to the geometric quadrume \(\mathcal{V}(\gamma)\): \[\chi_p(\mathcal{V}(\gamma)) = -\varepsilon(\gamma)\] This provides an exact, discrete realization of the Bateman–Turok ghost-parity mechanism, decoupling negative-norm ghost states in discrete quantum mechanics without requiring continuous limits.

6. Conclusion: The Geometry Was There All Along

The next time you see a conic section being manipulated by an orthogonal matrix on a chalkboard, take a closer look.

The algebraic trick of factoring \(z^2 - y^2 = (z-y)(z+y)\), adding a projective coordinate at infinity, and extending numbers by \(\sqrt{-1}\) is not just high-school algebra or a curiosity of complex analysis. It is the very mathematical scaffolding that allows relativistic physics, spinor double-covers, and quantum holonomies to be computed with exact, rational precision inside \(2 \times 2\) matrices.
