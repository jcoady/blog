The starting question

A pure qubit state is a point on the Bloch sphere, a copy of \(\mathbb{CP}^1 \cong S^2\). Take three such states with Bloch vectors \(n_1, n_2, n_3\). One quantity you can attach to them is the Bargmann invariant

\[ \Delta_3 = \langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle\langle\psi_2|\psi_3\rangle\langle\psi_3|\psi_1\rangle . \]

It does not change if you rephase any of the kets, so it depends only on the three states. Its modulus is a product of transition probabilities. When it is nonzero, its argument is half the oriented solid angle of the geodesic triangle on the sphere, which is the Pancharatnam–Berry geometric phase.

In terms of the Bloch vectors, a short Pauli-matrix calculation gives

\[ 4\Delta_3 = S + iV, \qquad S = 1 + c_{12} + c_{13} + c_{23}, \quad V = n_1\cdot(n_2\times n_3), \]

where \(c_{ij} = n_i\cdot n_j\).

The question behind this post, and the accompanying paper, is: which parts of \(\Delta_3\) can be seen by a purely projective geometry of the three axes, and which need extra data?

Two different “projectivizations”

This is where it is easy to get confused, so it is worth slowing down.

  • Quantum projectivization. A state is a ray in \(\mathbb{C}^2\). This identifies kets that differ by a phase and gives the Bloch sphere \(S^2\). The vector \(n\) is uniquely determined by the state.
  • Elliptic UHG projectivization. Wildberger’s universal hyperbolic geometry (UHG) works with points of the real projective plane \(\mathbb{RP}^2\), that is, lines through the origin in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Here \(n\) and \(-n\) are the same point.

These are not the same quotient. The state with Bloch vector \(-n\) is the one orthogonal to the state with Bloch vector \(n\). So passing to \(\mathbb{RP}^2\) is an additional quotient of the quantum picture, and each UHG triangle is the image of eight (generally different) physical triples of qubit states, one for each choice of signs \(n_i \mapsto \pm n_i\).

What UHG sees

In UHG the basic metrical quantities are quadrance and quadrea instead of distance and area. With the positive-definite form \(x^2+y^2+z^2\) (the elliptic case), for unit vectors:

\[ q_k = 1 - c_{ij}^2, \qquad \mathcal{A} = V^2 . \]

For a single pair of states with transition probability \(\pi\), the quadrance is

\[ q = 4\pi(1-\pi), \]

which is the second spread polynomial. It sees the double-angle quantity \(\sin^2\theta\), and it cannot tell \(\pi\) from \(1-\pi\). Identical and orthogonal states both have \(q = 0\).

Result 1: the quadrea and the half-turn product

In Wildberger’s quaternion framework for rotations, a pure unit quaternion \((0, n)\) represents the half turn about the axis \(n\). Multiply the three half turns:

\[ H = H_1H_2H_3 = \bigl(V,\; -c_{12}n_3 + c_{13}n_2 - c_{23}n_1\bigr), \qquad N(H) = 1 . \]

The scalar part is exactly the triple product \(V\). Since the half spread of the rotation satisfies \(1 - h = x^2/N\), this gives

\[ \boxed{\;\mathcal{A} = V^2 = 1 - h(H) = 16\,(\operatorname{Im}\Delta_3)^2\;} \]

So the elliptic UHG quadrea is the squared imaginary part of the Bargmann invariant, up to the factor 16, and it equals one minus the half spread of the rotation given by the product of the three half turns.

Result 2 (the main one): a quaternion that is the Bargmann invariant

The half-turn product only sees \(V\). To get the full complex number we need the real part \(S\) too. We now assume that no pair of the three states is orthogonal, that is \(c_{ij}\neq -1\). (If \(c_{ij}=-1\), the Bargmann invariant vanishes and the shortest geodesic between the two states is not unique.) For each edge, define

\[ E_{ij} = 1 - n_i n_j = (1 + c_{ij},\; n_i\times n_j). \]

Using the quaternion sandwich action, \(E_{ij}\) carries \(n_i\) to \(n_j\). Its scalar part \(1 + c_{ij}\) is positive, so it is the canonical positive-scalar representative of the shortest rotation from \(n_i\) to \(n_j\).

Now go once around the triangle:

\[ \boxed{\;K = E_{12}E_{23}E_{31} = 2\,(S,\; V n_1) = 8\,\Phi_{n_1}(\Delta_3)\;} \]

Here \(\Phi_{n_1}\) is the algebra isomorphism from \(\mathbb{C}\) onto the plane \(\langle 1, n_1\rangle \subset \mathbb{H}\) that sends \(i\) to \(n_1\). Since \(n_1^2 = -1\), the oriented axis \(n_1\) plays exactly the role of the imaginary unit.

The proof is a direct expansion. Multiplying out \((1 - n_1n_2)(1 - n_2n_3)(1 - n_3n_1)\) and using \(n_in_i = -1\), the pairwise terms combine into \(2S\) in the scalar part and the triple term gives \(2Vn_1\) in the vector part.

Normalized version

The \(E_{ij}\) are invertible quaternions but not unit quaternions: \(N(E_{ij}) = 2(1+c_{ij})\). Their product has \(N(K) = 8\Pi = 64\lvert\Delta_3\rvert^2\) with \(\Pi = (1+c_{12})(1+c_{13})(1+c_{23})\). Normalizing,

\[ \widehat{E}_{ij} = \frac{E_{ij}}{\sqrt{2(1+c_{ij})}}, \qquad \widehat{K} = \widehat{E}_{12}\widehat{E}_{23}\widehat{E}_{31} = \frac{K}{\sqrt{8\Pi}} = \Phi_{n_1}\!\left(\frac{\Delta_3}{\lvert\Delta_3\rvert}\right). \]

The \(\widehat{E}_{ij}\) and \(\widehat{K}\) are honest unit quaternions, that is, \(SU(2)\) elements, and they represent the same rotations as \(E_{ij}\) and \(K\). This normalized product is the right object for the Wilson-loop analogy below. The unnormalized \(K\) is what you want for exact rational arithmetic, since normalizing needs square roots.

What this does and does not say

  • The ordered product of edge transports around the triangle is a discrete holonomy. No continuous connection or gauge field is constructed.
  • The rotation of \(K\) fixes \(n_1\) and has angle \(2\arg\Delta_3 = \Omega\). But \(K\) and \(-K\) give the same rotation, so the rotation alone remembers only the doubled angle.
  • The sign of \(K\) (its positive ray), together with the oriented axis \(n_1\), remembers \(\arg\Delta_3\) modulo \(2\pi\).
  • Without \(n_1\), \(K\) cannot distinguish \(\Delta_3\) from its complex conjugate.

A worked example with rational numbers

Take

\[ n_1 = (1,0,0), \quad n_2 = \tfrac{1}{3}(2,2,1), \quad n_3 = \tfrac{1}{7}(2,3,6). \]

Then \(c_{12} = \tfrac23\), \(c_{13} = \tfrac27\), \(c_{23} = \tfrac{16}{21}\), \(V = \tfrac37\), and \(S = \tfrac{19}{7}\). So

\[ \Delta_3 = \frac{19 + 3i}{28}, \qquad K = \left(\tfrac{38}{7},\; \left(\tfrac{6}{7}, 0, 0\right)\right), \qquad \tan(\Omega/2) = \tfrac{3}{19}. \]

(As a rotation, \(K\) is only defined up to a nonzero scalar. Up to positive scaling it is the ray of \((19, 3, 0, 0)\), and the positive scaling is what keeps the sign information discussed above.)

The half-turn product is \(H = \tfrac{1}{21}(9, -16, -2, -10)\), with norm 1 and \(1 - h(H) = 81/441 = 9/49 = \mathcal{A}\), as predicted.

The hand-computed numbers above can be rechecked with exact rational arithmetic:

Code 
from fractions import Fraction as Fr

def dot(a, b):   return sum(x*y for x, y in zip(a, b))
def cross(a, b): return (a[1]*b[2]-a[2]*b[1], a[2]*b[0]-a[0]*b[2], a[0]*b[1]-a[1]*b[0])

def qmul(p, q):
    x, v = p; y, u = q
    c = cross(v, u)
    return (x*y - dot(v, u), tuple(x*u[i] + y*v[i] - c[i] for i in range(3)))

n1 = (Fr(1), Fr(0), Fr(0))
n2 = (Fr(2,3), Fr(2,3), Fr(1,3))
n3 = (Fr(2,7), Fr(3,7), Fr(6,7))

def E(a, b):                      # E_ij = 1 - n_i n_j
    s, v = qmul((Fr(0), a), (Fr(0), b))
    return (1 - s, tuple(-t for t in v))

K = qmul(qmul(E(n1, n2), E(n2, n3)), E(n3, n1))
print(K)   # expect (38/7, (6/7, 0, 0)) = 8*Phi(Delta_3)

A Wilson loop on a triangle

Physicists will recognize the structure. A Wilson loop is the gauge-invariant trace of a path-ordered product of transports around a closed loop, and both \(\Delta_3\) and \(\widehat{K}\) are discrete versions of that.

Abelian case. Assume the overlaps are nonzero and take the phase of each overlap as a \(U(1)\) link variable,

\[ U_{ij}=\frac{\langle\psi_i|\psi_j\rangle}{|\langle\psi_i|\psi_j\rangle|}. \]

Rephasing the kets changes these links by a gauge transformation, and the product \(U_{12}U_{23}U_{31}=\Delta_3/|\Delta_3|\) is gauge invariant. So \(\Delta_3\) is a triangular plaquette for the Berry (Pancharatnam) connection. For the standard Berry connection on the tautological line bundle over \(\mathbb{CP}^1\), a fine-mesh limit gives the continuum Wilson loop \(\exp\bigl(i\oint A\bigr)\), which for a spin-½ state is \(e^{\pm i\Omega/2}\). This continuum construction is standard background. It is not carried out in the accompanying paper.

Non-abelian case. The normalized quaternion \(\widehat{K}=\widehat{E}_{12}\widehat{E}_{23}\widehat{E}_{31}\) is a path-ordered product of \(SU(2)\) link variables, each carrying \(n_i\) to \(n_j\) along a great-circle arc. Going once around the triangle gives a rotation about \(n_1\) by the enclosed solid angle \(\Omega\), up to orientation convention. Wilson loops are traces, and the paper’s quantities are traces:

  • In the spin-½ representation the trace is \(2\cos(\Omega/2)\), twice the scalar part of \(\widehat{K}\).
  • In the spin-1 (rotation matrix) representation, \(\operatorname{tr}\mathcal{R}(K)=1+2\cos\Omega\). Since the half spread is \(h=(3-\operatorname{tr}\mathcal{R})/4=\sin^2(\Omega/2)\), the half spread is an affine function of the spin-1 Wilson loop around the triangle.
Where the analogy stops

The paper constructs no connection and takes no continuum limit, which is why it says “discrete holonomy”. There is no spacetime or dynamics here: the “gauge field” lives on the Bloch sphere, which is a parameter space. And the UHG triangle forgets the signs of the \(n_i\), so it does not determine the Wilson loop. Different sign choices give up to four values of \(K\). I have not checked the sign conventions of the spin-½ trace against the paper’s orientation conventions.

A nontrivial phase of \(\Delta_3\) is a nontrivial holonomy around this discrete loop. It obstructs making all three overlaps simultaneously real and positive by rephasing the three states. This is compatible with the Berry connection on the tautological line bundle over \(\mathbb{CP}^1\), but the global topology of that bundle is a separate fact. Nontrivial holonomy around one loop does not by itself show that a bundle is topologically nontrivial, since a connection on a trivial bundle can also have nontrivial holonomy.

The eight sign choices

Replace \(n_i\) by \(\varepsilon_i n_i\) with \(\varepsilon_i = \pm 1\). The UHG triangle does not change, but the physical states do. Writing \(\varepsilon = \varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2\varepsilon_3\):

Quantity Behaviour over the 8 sign choices
quadrances \(q_k\), \(P = c_{12}c_{13}c_{23}\), quadrea \(\mathcal{A} = V^2\) invariant
rotation of \(H_1H_2H_3\) invariant
positive ray of \(H\) changes by the factor \(\varepsilon\)
\(S\), \(\lvert\Delta_3\rvert^2\) at most four values (generically four)
\(\Delta_3\) at most eight values (generically eight), in conjugate pairs
quaternion \(K\) at most four values; unchanged by the global flip

The global flip \((\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,\varepsilon_3) \mapsto (-\varepsilon_1,-\varepsilon_2,-\varepsilon_3)\) leaves every \(E_{ij}\) and \(K\) unchanged, but conjugates \(\Delta_3\) and reverses \(n_1\). This is consistent with \(K = 8\Phi_{n_1}(\Delta_3)\) because \(\Phi_{-n_1}(\overline{\Delta_3}) = \Phi_{n_1}(\Delta_3)\).

For the example above, the four values of \(S'\) are \(\tfrac{19}{7}\), \(\tfrac{17}{21}\), \(-\tfrac{1}{7}\) and \(\tfrac{13}{21}\). The same UHG triangle therefore carries four different moduli and eight different Bargmann values. This is the precise sense in which the elliptic projective geometry forgets the phase.

An arithmetic twist over the rationals

When the Bloch vectors have rational coordinates, every quantity above is rational, so a natural question is whether the rotation of \(K\) can be written with a norm-one rational quaternion. Over the reals every nonzero quaternion can be normalized. Over \(\mathbb{Q}\) it usually cannot, because normalizing needs a square root.

The answer is clean. Two rational quaternions giving the same rotation differ by a rational scalar (the centre of the quaternion algebra), so:

The rotation has a norm-one rational representative if and only if \(N(K) = 64\lvert\Delta_3\rvert^2\) is a rational square, equivalently \(\lvert\Delta_3\rvert^2\) is a nonzero rational square.

In the example, \(\lvert\Delta_3\rvert^2 = 370/784\), and \(370 = 2\cdot 5\cdot 37\) is not a square. So the holonomy rotation is a perfectly good rational rotation, with rational quaternion representative \(K\), but no rational unit quaternion represents it. The projective description reaches it. The normalized one does not, and \(\widehat{K}\) from the Wilson-loop section has irrational entries here.

Where Freed’s slogan fits

The motivation for the project is Dan Freed’s thesis in What is an anomaly?: quantum theory is projective; quantization is linear. Physical content lives on projective objects, and a symmetry group acts only up to a central extension. Several different “lifts” are in play here, and they should not be mixed up:

  1. Scalar quotient. \(F^\times \to \mathbb{H}_F^\times \to \mathbb{H}_F^\times/F^\times \cong SO(3,F)\). This has the same pattern as \(\mathbb{C}^\times \to GL \to PGL\), but over \(\mathbb{R}\) the Euclidean quaternions are a division algebra, so the group is \(SO(3)\), not \(PGL_2(\mathbb{R})\).
  2. Positive-ray double cover. Over an ordered field, quaternion positive rays map two-to-one onto rotations. Over \(\mathbb{R}\) this is the familiar double covering \(SU(2)\to SO(3)\) with kernel \(\{\pm 1\}\), a genuine topological covering. It is a different thing from the central \(U(1)\)-extension \(U(2) \to SO(3)\).
  3. Spinor norm. The square class of the quaternion norm is a homomorphism to an abelian group, and it measures whether a rational rotation lifts to a rational norm-one quaternion. It is not an extension class of the kind Freed uses.

There is also a pleasant finite-dimensional echo of Freed’s line-valued amplitudes. If \(L_i\) is the line of the state \(\psi_i\), the overlap \(\langle\psi_i|\psi_j\rangle\) is a map \(L_j \to L_i\) (in unit frames), and the cyclic composite \(L_1\to L_3\to L_2\to L_1\) is an endomorphism of a line, canonically a number: \(\Delta_3\).

Does Freed mention the Bargmann invariant?

No. Freed’s paper does not mention the Bargmann invariant or the Berry phase, and Bargmann does not appear in his reference list. The connection in this post is my own framing, not something he states. It runs like this.

  • “Quantum theory is projective.” Overlaps \(\langle\psi_i|\psi_j\rangle\) only make sense as maps between lines, so they are not numbers until you choose unit vectors. Phase-independent data are the closed loops of such maps. \(\Delta_3\) is the simplest one.
  • “Quantization is linear.” To work with actual numbers you must trivialize the lines, that is, choose phases for the kets. For three states with nonzero overlaps, this can be done so that all three overlaps are real and positive exactly when \(\Delta_3\) is real and positive. A nontrivial phase of \(\Delta_3\) is therefore an obstruction to a consistent choice of phases. This is the same kind of “descent requires a trivialization” argument that Freed makes for the anomaly, but at the level of three points, with no field theory involved.
What this is not

The paper does not construct an anomaly, a gerbe, or any field-theoretic object. The relation to Freed is motivational: a concrete illustration of what is visible projectively and what needs a lift. Nothing here shows that the Bargmann invariant is an anomaly in his sense.

Caveats and honest limitations

Status of the work
  • Only qubits are treated. Higher-dimensional systems (\(\mathbb{CP}^n\) with \(n>1\)) are not captured by \(\mathbb{RP}^2\).
  • The hyperbolic form \(x^2+y^2-z^2\) (null points, boosts, the split algebra \(M_2(F)\)) has no quantum interpretation in this work, and I do not know whether the half-turn identity has a Lorentzian analogue.
  • The identities were checked by hand and on rational examples. An independent symbolic verification would be a sensible next step.
  • The surjectivity of the projective quaternion parametrization onto \(SO(3,F)\) is cited from an unpublished draft by Wildberger, Rational Rotations of Three Dimensional Euclidean Space. The sandwich formula, multiplicativity and injectivity used in the calculations are proved in the paper’s appendix.
  • The classical ingredients (the Gram determinant identity, the Bargmann–Pancharatnam phase, quaternionic spherical excess) are not new. Any novelty lies in the packaging: elliptic UHG, projective quaternions, and the explicit accounting of what survives the sign choices.

Takeaways

  • The elliptic UHG triangle of three Bloch axes remembers the quadrances and the quadrea, subject to the triple product relation. It forgets the orientations of the vectors and the full Bargmann phase.
  • The identity \(E_{12}E_{23}E_{31} = 8\,\Phi_{n_1}(\Delta_3)\) gives an exact quaternionic realization of the Bargmann invariant, with the oriented base axis supplying the complex structure.
  • The eight sign choices describe exactly what the extra \(\mathbb{RP}^2\) quotient loses.
  • Over \(\mathbb{Q}\), the difference between a rational projective rotation and a rational norm-one quaternion is measured by a square class, and the Bargmann modulus decides it for the holonomy rotation.
