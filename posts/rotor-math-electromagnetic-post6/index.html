Why this post

Five posts in, the work has produced several results that each hold up on their own but have not yet been joined into one system. This post is a map: what the programme set out to do, which parts are established, which are choices, which are negative results, and what the central missing piece is. It contains no new calculations. It does record one corrected script and a short log of corrections made along the way.

The goal, in three questions

  1. Representation. What can rotor mathematics already say about electromagnetism and abelian gauge theory?
  2. Evolution. How do the mathematical objects change consistently from one time step to the next?
  3. Derivation. Can that evolution be derived from the rotor framework itself, instead of borrowed from standard electromagnetism or lattice gauge theory?

The first question has produced real results. The second has produced useful building blocks. The third is the unfinished one.

What the lecture asks for

Lecture 13 of the particle physics course (handwritten notes) works with three objects, easiest to picture on a grid:

  • the matter field \(\psi(x)\), the state of a charged particle at each point;
  • the connection \(A_\mu(x)\), which says how to compare the state at one point with the state at a neighbouring point (on a lattice, a link variable on each edge);
  • the curvature \(F_{\mu\nu}\), what is left when a state is carried around a small loop and does not come back unchanged (\([D_\mu,D_\nu]\psi=iqF_{\mu\nu}\psi\)).

Knowing the field strength does not, by itself, give the connection, and neither tells you how the fields change in time. That is why the series moved from representing the field (Part 1) to links, matter and dynamics (Parts 2–5).

Three results that shape the next step

  1. The links are chosen. Turning a potential into Cayley links uses \(a_e=-\tfrac12e\int_e\mathbf A\), a regularisation. The potential itself is not intrinsic to \(\zeta\) either (it depends on a gauge choice). Neither is derived from the rotor algebra.
  2. A rational, exactly gauge-equivariant link prescription cannot exist in the sense of Proposition 20(c): if gauge transformations act on links through the Cayley group law by some map \(\gamma\) of the endpoint gauge parameters, for all rational gauge parameters, then the links are gauge invariant, which a link cannot be. The only exact exception is quantized flux (integer multiples of a fixed Cayley unit). This does not rule out constructions with extra variables or a different transformation law. Computing the flux-first face parameter \(-\tfrac12e\Phi_f\) directly does not even give realizable faces: the \(\oplus\) cube identity fails at \(O(e^3)\).
  3. Exactness is limited. The Cayley time step commutes exactly with the exact evolution only for polynomial solutions of total degree \(\le2\) in linear integrated variables; the degree-3 examples tested fail. Elsewhere it holds with measured errors (\(O(k^2)\), \(O(\tau^2)\), and \(O(\epsilon^2)\) for the weak-field comparison with Part 1’s compact dynamics).

A fourth item is a warning rather than a theorem. The attempt to keep every variable group-valued and update by group translations (Part 4, Calculation 16) passes reversibility and Gauss checks that any abelian group would pass, and it has the wrong wave speed and no usable step size. Reversibility and a conserved Gauss functional are not evidence of Maxwell dynamics.

Statements this series does not make

  • It does not derive dynamics from projective geometry alone. The free equation of Calculation 17 assumes a first-order, linear, \(K\)-linear equation and takes the rotor action on \(\zeta\) (a specified boost generator) as input.
  • It does not show that the Gauss law is preserved with sources. What has been shown is that the Gauss law is conserved for static charges in the compact dynamics of Part 1 (Calculation 10), and that the Gauss functional is conserved by the free linear Cayley step of Part 4 (Calculation 18). A time-dependent current needs the matching continuity equation and a coupled system, and the Wilson–Dirac current of Part 3 has not been coupled to either update.
  • It does not show that the links built in Part 5 represent electromagnetic transport derived from the rotor.
  • It does not treat sources, a Minkowski matter operator, or Lorentz covariance on the lattice.

The missing piece, in plain language

Picture a grid of little squares. The rotor anchor describes the field. Each square has a flux through it. Each edge carries a link describing how a phase is transported along it. The product of the four links around a square is its holonomy, the curvature of that square. Charged matter moves on the grid and produces a current, and the field must respond to that current.

What is missing is a single justified rule connecting these objects. Three questions make it precise:

  1. Given the rotor anchor, how do we obtain the links, or at least their plaquette holonomies, without inserting a separate connection by hand?
  2. When the rotor field evolves, do the links and holonomies evolve consistently with it?
  3. When matter moves, does its current enter the same update, so that gauge covariance, charge continuity and Gauss’s law fit together?

The key missing result is a compatibility theorem connecting rotor evolution, link evolution, curvature and the matter current. It is not just another dynamical equation.

Where to go next

I agree with narrowing the work to the central bridge instead of adding another matter model or another solver. The equivariance no-go changes how that bridge should be formulated, though. “Derive exact gauge-equivariant links from \(\zeta\) for general fields” cannot succeed over \(\mathbb Q\). Three routes remain:

  • Quantized fluxes. Here an exactly equivariant chain exists statically. The open question is whether any dynamics preserves quantization while approximating Maxwell. My suspicion, which is a conjecture and not a result, is that exactness and continuity pull against each other: the only exact group-valued dynamics tested so far (Calculation 16) failed the Maxwell tests.
  • Group-valued links as primary data, with an action principle. Define the dynamics on Cayley links directly, as in Part 1’s compact dynamics, and ask what rotor structure determines the action. A concrete candidate: the Lorentz-invariant \(\mathrm{Re}(\zeta\cdot\zeta)=E^2-B^2\) is the Maxwell Lagrangian density, so a spacetime plaquette action in Cayley variables, with spacelike and timelike planes entering with opposite signs, would tie the rotor invariant to the lattice action. The invariant alone is not enough: the action must also specify the independent dynamical variables, and variation must be shown to give the intended equations. Adding the Wilson–Dirac term might then supply the matter current as the source, and gauge invariance gives identities and constraints, but whether a correct sourced lattice Gauss law follows depends on the time discretisation, the temporal links, the matter action and the definition of the charge density. That is a programme to be demonstrated, not an automatic consequence. This route needs temporal links and a real-time Dirac operator, neither of which exists yet in this series.
  • Leading-order closure. Accept equivariance only to leading order in \(e\) and prove explicit error bounds, so the chain is stated as exact up to a controlled remainder.

I would try the action-principle route first, because it addresses both the missing derivation of the evolution and the matter coupling, and because working with group-valued links natively avoids the specific obstruction of Proposition 20(c). It is not known to succeed. The quantized case is worth a separate small exploration.

The next task, stated narrowly

The next step is not “build a complete rotor–electromagnetic theory”. It is to answer five questions in a small model (for example, one space dimension plus time with a U(1) link):

  1. What are the independent dynamical variables?
  2. What is the exact discrete action?
  3. What equations follow from varying the links and the matter variables?
  4. Does the resulting system satisfy the intended gauge and charge-conservation identities?
  5. Where precisely does the rotor anchor enter, and what is actually derived from it?

If these can be answered, that is a genuine advance toward the compatibility theorem. If they cannot, the failure should identify a more precise obstruction.

Summary

The series started by showing that rotor mathematics can represent electromagnetic field strength. The later posts tried to show that it can also generate the gauge transport and the consistent time evolution that turn that representation into a dynamical theory. Important pieces are established, several are chosen and marked as such, one is a proved obstruction, and the pieces have not yet been shown to fit together. The work has narrowed the problem: we now know which identities hold, which constructions are separate, and where an asserted connection would need an actual proof.
