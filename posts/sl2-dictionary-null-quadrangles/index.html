Points as Matrices: A Constructive sl(2) Dictionary for Null Quadrangles

Wildberger’s identification of projective points with trace-zero sl(2) matrices turns UHG’s join operation into the literal Lie bracket and its quadrance into the trace form – verified as exact identities – and shows the Jacobi identity is the reason three altitudes of a nil-triangle always meet.

Picking up where we left off

This is the sixth post in the series exploring the algebra hiding inside a very simple picture: four null points on a conic, and the seven-point figure their diagonal triangle generates.

  • Posts 1-2 built the diagonal triangle and its outward triangulation, producing a six-element anharmonic orbit of a cross-ratio \(q\).
  • Posts 3-4 found a trace-free triple \((q_1,q_2,q_3)\) in this orbit, showed the anharmonic \(S_3\) acts on it by exact coordinate permutation, and used Schur’s lemma and Serre’s theorem to identify the resulting Lie algebra as \(\mathfrak{sl}_3(\mathbb{C})/\mathfrak{su}(3)\).
  • Post 5 pinned down an explicit dictionary between the abstract parameter \(q\) and the raw cross-ratio \(\lambda\) of a concretely coordinatized null quadrangle.

Every one of those results was earned by matching invariants: the Cartan matrix, the Casimirs, the classification. None of them constructed an actual Lie bracket out of the geometry’s own operations. This post closes that specific gap for the rank-1 case, using a construction from N. J. Wildberger’s UHG lecture series that identifies projective points with trace-zero \(2\times2\) matrices, join with the Lie bracket, and reflection with conjugation:

Points as matrices

The ambient space of UHG’s projective plane is \(\mathbb{R}^{2,1}\), with quadratic form \(Q(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2-z^2\). Identify a vector \((x,y,z)\) with the trace-zero matrix

\[ M(x,y,z) = \begin{pmatrix} x & y+z \\ y-z & -x \end{pmatrix} \in \mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{R}). \]

A direct computation gives \(\det M(v) = -Q(v)\), so null points correspond exactly to nilpotent matrices — the null circle is the nilpotent cone of \(\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{R})\), viewed through its own trace form. Every one of this series’ null points \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_4\) is, under this dictionary, a literal nilpotent element of a Lie algebra, not merely a point satisfying an algebraic condition that happens to resemble one.

Join is the Lie bracket, quadrance is the trace form

Computing the commutator directly: \[ [M(a),M(b)] = -2\,M\big(\mathrm{join}(a,b)\big) \] holds as an exact symbolic identity for arbitrary points \(a,b\) — a fixed scalar multiple across all three coordinates, which is all projective equivalence requires. Likewise, \[ Q(v) = \tfrac12\,\mathrm{tr}\big(M(v)^2\big), \qquad B(u,v) = \tfrac12\,\mathrm{tr}\big(M(u)M(v)\big) \] reproduce UHG’s quadratic and bilinear forms with zero symbolic discrepancy. So the trace form on \(\mathfrak{sl}_2\) isn’t merely proportional to the UHG metric — under this identification, it is the UHG metric.

This survives the full iterated construction. Building the diagonal-triangle vertex \(e\) two ways — via UHG’s join formula, and via the nested Lie bracket \(e_{\text{lie}} = [[M(\alpha_1),M(\alpha_2)],[M(\alpha_3),M(\alpha_4)]]\) — gives the same projective point (a fixed scalar ratio of \(-8\) across all coordinates, for the \(\lambda\)-parametrized quadrangle from Post 5). Computing the fully-right property purely from the trace form on these Lie-bracket points reproduces \[ q(e_{\text{lie}},f_{\text{lie}}) = q(f_{\text{lie}},g_{\text{lie}}) = q(e_{\text{lie}},g_{\text{lie}}) = 1 \] exactly, using nothing but matrix multiplication and trace — no UHG-specific machinery at all.

The Jacobi identity proves the orthocenter exists

This dictionary gives a genuinely constructive proof of a classical UHG fact: three altitudes of a triangle are concurrent. The altitude from \(p\) in the triangle \(pqr\) is \(N_p = \mathrm{join}(p,\ \mathrm{join}(q,r))\) (using the self-duality of pole and polar). Translating into brackets, \(N_p \leftrightarrow [M(p),[M(q),M(r)]]\), and cyclically for \(N_q, N_r\). The Jacobi identity, \[ [X,[Y,Z]] + [Y,[Z,X]] + [Z,[X,Y]] = 0, \] with \(X=M(p), Y=M(q), Z=M(r)\), says exactly that \[ N_p + N_q + N_r = 0. \] Three lines with homogeneous coordinate vectors summing to zero are automatically concurrent — this is precisely the linear-dependence condition for three lines to share a point. So the orthocenter’s existence isn’t a separate fact to prove case by case; it is the Jacobi identity, restated in projective coordinates.

I verified this two ways. First, symbolically, for fully generic points \(p,q,r\): \(N_p+N_q+N_r=(0,0,0)\) exactly, both via the UHG join formula and via the Lie bracket. Second, on the genuine (non-degenerate) orthocenters \(h_1,h_2,h_3\) from the four-null-point configuration of the companion paper Invariant Quadrea of Orthocenter Triangles: the same identity holds, confirming concurrence on a real example rather than only in the abstract.

One instructive pitfall along the way: applying this check to the diagonal triangle \(e,f,g\) instead gives \(N_e=N_f=N_g=(0,0,0)\) individually — a trivial, vacuous confirmation. That triangle is self-polar (Post 1), so each vertex’s polar is the opposite side already, meaning the altitude construction degenerates to \(\mathrm{join}\) of a point with a scalar multiple of itself, which is always zero. Self-polar triangles are exactly the case where this construction is not meaningful — worth flagging, since it’s an easy way to convince yourself a check has passed when it has actually failed to run at all.

What this does, and does not, establish

This is a genuine, load-bearing upgrade for the rank-1 part of the story. The entire ambient space of every construction in this series — all four null points, the diagonal triangle, the outward triangulation — sits inside \(\mathrm{ad}(\mathfrak{sl}_2)\), the adjoint representation of \(\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{R})\), with join realized as the actual bracket and quadrance as the actual trace form. That is a constructive fact, not a classificatory one: no appeal to Serre’s theorem or an abstract uniqueness argument was needed, only direct computation.

It says nothing new about \(\mathfrak{su}(3)\). \(\mathfrak{sl}_2\) is rank 1; the Cartan matrix from Posts 3-4 is rank 2. Nothing here manufactures an 8-dimensional space or a genuine \(\mathfrak{sl}_3\) bracket out of the geometry — the earlier Serre’s-theorem link (which identifies which Lie algebra the invariant data belongs to, without constructing it) remains the only bridge to \(\mathfrak{su}(3)\) established so far. Whether the trace-free triples found since Post 4 can be built the same constructive way — as an explicit combination of iterated \(\mathfrak{sl}_2\) brackets landing in a genuine \(\mathfrak{sl}_3\) Cartan element, rather than packaged by hand into a diagonal matrix — remains open.

Check it yourself

from sympy import symbols, simplify, Matrix, Rational

x1,y1,z1,x2,y2,z2,x3,y3,z3 = symbols('x1 y1 z1 x2 y2 z2 x3 y3 z3')
p, q, r = (x1,y1,z1), (x2,y2,z2), (x3,y3,z3)

def join(a, b):
    xa,ya,za = a; xb,yb,zb = b
    return (ya*zb - yb*za, za*xb - zb*xa, xb*ya - xa*yb)

def M(v):
    x, y, z = v
    return Matrix([[x, y+z], [y-z, -x]])

def vec_from_M(mat):
    x = mat[0,0]; s = mat[0,1]; d = mat[1,0]
    return (x, (s+d)/2, (s-d)/2)

def bracket_vec(u, v):
    return tuple(simplify(c) for c in vec_from_M(simplify(M(u)*M(v) - M(v)*M(u))))

# join vs bracket
Ma, Mb = M(p), M(q)
bracket = simplify(Ma*Mb - Mb*Ma)
w = tuple(simplify(c) for c in vec_from_M(bracket))
j = join(p, q)
print("bracket -> vector:", w)
print("join(p,q):        ", j)

# Q and B via trace form
def Q(v):
    x, y, z = v
    return x**2 + y**2 - z**2

def Q_trace(v):
    return simplify(Rational(1,2)*(M(v)*M(v)).trace())

def B_uhg(u, v):
    xu,yu,zu = u; xv,yv,zv = v
    return xu*xv + yu*yv - zu*zv

def B_trace(u, v):
    return simplify(Rational(1,2)*(M(u)*M(v)).trace())

print("\nQ_trace(p) - Q(p) =", simplify(Q_trace(p) - Q(p)))
print("B_trace(p,q) - B_uhg(p,q) =", simplify(B_trace(p,q) - B_uhg(p,q)))

# Jacobi identity => altitude concurrence
def altitude(a, b, c):
    return join(a, join(b, c))

Np, Nq, Nr = altitude(p,q,r), altitude(q,r,p), altitude(r,p,q)
total = tuple(simplify(Np[i] + Nq[i] + Nr[i]) for i in range(3))
print("\nN_p + N_q + N_r =", total, " (Jacobi identity)")
bracket -> vector: (-2*y1*z2 + 2*y2*z1, 2*x1*z2 - 2*x2*z1, 2*x1*y2 - 2*x2*y1)
join(p,q):         (y1*z2 - y2*z1, -x1*z2 + x2*z1, -x1*y2 + x2*y1)

Q_trace(p) - Q(p) = 0
B_trace(p,q) - B_uhg(p,q) = 0

N_p + N_q + N_r = (0, 0, 0)  (Jacobi identity)

The bracket and join agree up to the constant factor \(-2\) across every coordinate, the trace form matches UHG’s metric exactly, and the Jacobi identity produces concurrence directly — all as symbolic identities, holding for arbitrary points, not special cases.

Where this leaves us

The rank-1 backbone of this entire series — every join, every quadrance, every concurrence argument — now has a fully constructive home inside \(\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{R})\) and its adjoint representation, verified rather than assumed. The rank-2 story from Posts 3-4 still rests on classification (Serre’s theorem) rather than construction. Closing that gap — finding an explicit combination of iterated \(\mathfrak{sl}_2\) brackets that produces a genuine \(\mathfrak{sl}_3\) Cartan element, rather than one assembled by hand from three separately-computed quadrances — remains the open problem this series has not yet solved.
