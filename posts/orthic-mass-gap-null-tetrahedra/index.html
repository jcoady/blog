Introduction

This post is a direct sequel to Invariant Quadrea of Orthocenter Triangles from Four Null Points in Universal Hyperbolic Geometry. That post stayed in the projective plane \(\mathbb{P}^2\): four null points on the absolute conic, four triply-nil-triangle orthocenters, and a single absolute invariant — the quadrea \(\mathcal{A} = 16/27\) — that turned out to be completely independent of how the four points were chosen.

Here we lift the same question one dimension higher. Instead of a quadrangle of four null points in \(\mathbb{P}^2\), we start with a null tetrahedron: four null points in \(\mathbb{P}^3\), each face of which is a triply nil triangle. Each face has its own orthocenter, and the four face-orthocenters form a new tetrahedron — the orthic tetrahedron. The question this time is not “what constant does the invariant equal” but “how much can it vary, and is there a floor below which it cannot go?”

The answer is yes: the common face quadrea \(A_{\text{orthic}}\) of the orthic tetrahedron is confined to the half-open interval \[ A_{\text{orthic}} \in \left[\, \tfrac{5}{54}, \; \tfrac{16}{27} \,\right), \] with the lower bound attained only at a distinguished, maximally symmetric configuration. Because this lower bound behaves like a floor that no configuration can cross — a persistent, irreducible minimum forced purely by the algebra of the construction — it is natural to call it an orthic mass gap, by loose analogy with the way a mass gap in a variational or quantum field theory marks the energy of the ground state above vacuum. Nothing physical is being claimed; it is a descriptive name for a strict positive lower bound on a geometric invariant.

Reassuringly, the upper bound recovers the previous post’s result exactly: as the null tetrahedron degenerates so that its fourth vertex becomes coplanar with the other three, the orthic tetrahedron flattens into the orthic quadrangle of the planar construction, and \(A_{\text{orthic}}\) approaches \(16/27\) — the constant from the \(\mathbb{P}^2\) theorem, recovered here as a supremum rather than an exact value.

Why the Null Tetrahedron Itself Has No Metric

In Universal Hyperbolic Geometry (UHG), as developed by N. J. Wildberger, quadrance between two points \(v, w\) is defined as \[ q(v,w) = 1 - \frac{B(v,w)^2}{Q(v)\,Q(w)}, \] where \(Q\) is the governing quadratic form and \(B\) its associated bilinear form. This expression is simply undefined whenever \(Q(v) = 0\) or \(Q(w) = 0\) — that is, whenever either point is null. Since a null tetrahedron \(\overline{\alpha_1\alpha_2\alpha_3\alpha_4}\) consists of four null points by definition, none of its six pairwise quadrances exist. There is no sense in which such a tetrahedron can be called “regular,” “isosceles,” or anything else metrical — its only well-defined structure is purely projective (its faces, its edges as lines, and their poles and polars).

To recover a metrical object from a null tetrahedron, the paper applies the orthocenter construction facewise. Each face \(\overline{\alpha_i\alpha_j\alpha_k}\) is a triply nil triangle, and although none of its vertices carry a quadrance, its orthocenter is still well-defined: it is built purely from joins, meets, and polars, all of which are rational projective operations that never require dividing by \(Q\) of a null point. This is the construction recalled in Figure 1 of the paper (and worked out in detail in the \(\mathbb{P}^2\) post linked above): each altitude \(N_i\) is the join of vertex \(\alpha_i\) with the pole \(L_i^{\perp}\) of the opposite side’s polar line \(L_i\), and the three altitudes concur at a single orthocenter \(h\).

Applying this facewise to a null tetrahedron in \(\mathbb{P}^3\) produces four orthocenters \(h_1, h_2, h_3, h_4\) — one per face, omitting the opposite vertex each time — and these four points generically have nonzero \(Q\). They therefore support ordinary quadrances, spreads, and quadreas. This is the orthic tetrahedron \(\overline{h_1h_2h_3h_4}\): a genuinely metrical object extracted, by a purely projective procedure, from a null configuration that itself admits no metric at all.

Face Invariance and Projective Exhaustion

Before any bound can be proved, two structural facts need to be established.

Lemma 1 — Face Invariance

Each face of the orthic tetrahedron is a triangle whose three vertices are themselves orthocenters — rational projective expressions in the four original null vertices \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3, \alpha_4\). The quadrea of a triangle is \[ \mathcal{A}(v_1,v_2,v_3) = \frac{q_1q_2q_3 - q_1b_{23}^2 - q_2b_{31}^2 - q_3 b_{12}^2 + 2b_{12}b_{23}b_{31}}{q_1q_2q_3}, \qquad q_i = Q(v_i),\ \ b_{ij} = B(v_i,v_j), \] so the quadrea of any orthic face is, after substitution, a rational function of the bilinear products \(B(\alpha_i,\alpha_j)\) between the original null vertices. Since both the orthocenter construction and the quadrea formula are invariant under relabeling the four vertices, the resulting rational expression is identical (up to relabeling) no matter which face of the orthic tetrahedron is chosen. All four faces therefore share one common quadrea, denoted \(A_{\text{orthic}}\) — the single scalar invariant this whole post is about.

Lemma 2 — Projective Exhaustion

The group \(PO(3,1)\) — the projective orthogonal group preserving \(Q\) — acts transitively on ordered triples of null points in general position. So, up to a global projective transformation, three of the four vertices of any null tetrahedron can always be moved to a fixed standard configuration, leaving only the fourth vertex free to range over the absolute quadric. Because \(A_{\text{orthic}}\) is built from \(PO(3,1)\)-invariant rational quantities, its value on this reduced, one-parameter-family (really two-parameter, via a stereographic pair \((r,s)\)) is representative of the entire moduli space of null tetrahedra. This is precisely the same reduction-to-normal-form strategy used in the \(\mathbb{P}^2\) post’s cross-ratio parametrization, just one projective dimension up: there, three null points were fixed and a single cross-ratio \(\lambda\) parametrized the fourth; here, three null points are fixed and a pair of stereographic parameters \((r,s)\) parametrizes the fourth.

The Orthic Mass Gap Theorem

With the invariant reduced to a function \(A_{\text{orthic}}(r,s)\) of two real parameters, the paper’s central result is:

Theorem 1 (Orthic Quadrea Range). For any nondegenerate null tetrahedron in \(\mathbb{P}^3\):

  1. \(A_{\text{orthic}} \ge 5/54\), with equality if and only if the null tetrahedron is orthically regular (up to projective equivalence).
  2. \(A_{\text{orthic}} < 16/27\), where \(16/27\) is a supremum, never attained by a genuine tetrahedron.

Definition 2 (Orthically Regular). A null tetrahedron is called orthically regular when its associated orthic tetrahedron has all six of its pairwise quadrances equal — i.e. the orthic tetrahedron is a regular tetrahedron in the ordinary UHG metrical sense. Because quadrance is undefined for the null tetrahedron itself, “regularity” can only ever be a statement about the orthic tetrahedron; the null tetrahedron only ever has the symmetric position in the underlying projective parameter space that produces this regularity.

The lower bound: a geometric mass gap

The value \(5/54\) is attained at exactly one point of the (projectively reduced) parameter space — the fully symmetric configuration where the fourth null vertex sits at the parameter value \[ r = s = \frac{\sqrt{3}-1}{2} \] in the gauge-fixed frame used in the paper’s verification script (vertices \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3\) pinned to a standard triple, \(\alpha_4\) free). At this point the orthic tetrahedron is a genuine regular tetrahedron, and moving away from it in any direction strictly increases \(A_{\text{orthic}}\). Figures 4 and 5 of the paper illustrate this: a smooth convex bowl in the surface plot, with concentric, roughly circular level sets in the contour map, both centered on the single minimum at \(5/54\).

This is the sense in which \(5/54\) is a mass gap: it is not merely the infimum of a set that never achieves it (as \(16/27\) turns out to be), but a value that is achieved, uniquely, at a distinguished symmetric ground state, with every other configuration sitting strictly above it. The analogy to a mass gap in a variational or field-theoretic setting — a strictly positive minimum separating a symmetric ground state from the rest of the spectrum — is only structural, not physical, but the resemblance is genuinely there in the shape of the invariant.

The supremum: planar degeneration

The upper bound behaves completely differently. As the fourth null vertex \(\alpha_4\) is pushed toward coplanarity with \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3\) — equivalently, as the Gram determinant of the null tetrahedron tends to zero — the tetrahedral structure itself collapses. In this limit the orthic tetrahedron flattens into a planar orthic quadrangle: exactly the configuration studied in the \(\mathbb{P}^2\) post, four null points on a conic in a projective plane together with the four orthocenters of their triply nil sub-triangles.

The diagonal triangle of that planar orthic quadrangle carries the fixed quadrea \(16/27\), proved in the earlier post as an absolute invariant independent of the cross-ratio \(\lambda\). Symbolic evaluation of the limit \(\alpha_4 \to\) (coplanar with \(\alpha_1\alpha_2\alpha_3)\) confirms that \(A_{\text{orthic}}\) converges to exactly this constant. Because the limit corresponds to a genuine degeneration — the null tetrahedron ceasing to be nondegenerate — \(16/27\) is a supremum, never actually reached by any honest, nondegenerate null tetrahedron. This is a satisfying consistency check across the two posts: the 3D invariant’s ceiling is precisely the 2D invariant’s fixed value, recovered as a limiting case rather than assumed.

Symbolic Verification

The paper backs the theorem with a SymPy script that (i) checks stationarity and positive-definite Hessian at the conjectured minimum, and (ii) checks the degeneration limit converges to \(16/27\). The script gauge-fixes three null vertices to a standard frame and lets the fourth vary over stereographic parameters \((r, s, u)\):

import sympy
from sympy import symbols, simplify, Matrix, diff, limit, sqrt

def verify_mass_gap():
    r, s, u = symbols('r s u', real=True)

    def B(P1, P2):
        return P1[0]*P2[0] + P1[1]*P2[1] + P1[2]*P2[2] - P1[3]*P2[3]

    def e(r_i, s_i, u_i):
        return Matrix([2*r_i*u_i, 2*s_i*u_i, u_i**2 - r_i**2 - s_i**2,
                        u_i**2 + r_i**2 + s_i**2])

    # Standard Frame (Gauge Fixing)
    A1, A2, A3 = e(0, 0, 1), e(1, 0, 1), e(0, 1, 1)
    A4 = e(r, s, u)

    def get_H(p1, p2, p3):
        return B(p2, p3)*p1 + B(p3, p1)*p2 + B(p1, p2)*p3

    def get_A(v1, v2, v3):
        q1, q2, q3 = B(v1, v1), B(v2, v2), B(v3, v3)
        b12, b23, b31 = B(v1, v2), B(v2, v3), B(v3, v1)
        return (q1*q2*q3 - q1*b23**2 - q2*b31**2 - q3*b12**2
                + 2*b12*b23*b31) / (q1*q2*q3)

    A_orthic = simplify(get_A(get_H(A1, A3, A4), get_H(A1, A2, A4), get_H(A1, A2, A3)))

    # 1. STATIONARITY & MINIMUM (r=s, u=1 normalized)
    r_c = (sqrt(3) - 1) / 2
    A_eval = A_orthic.subs({u: 1})
    q_min = simplify(A_eval.subs({r: r_c, s: r_c}))

    # 2. HESSIAN STABILITY
    Hxx = diff(A_eval, r, 2).subs({r: r_c, s: r_c})
    Hyy = diff(A_eval, s, 2).subs({r: r_c, s: r_c})
    Hxy = diff(A_eval, r, s).subs({r: r_c, s: r_c})
    det_H = simplify(Hxx*Hyy - Hxy**2)

    # 3. SUPREMUM (Degeneration Limit: r -> u)
    max_lim = simplify(limit(A_orthic.subs(s, r), r, u))

    print("Invariant is rational and symmetric under permutations.")
    print(f"Min Quadrea: {q_min}")        # 5/54
    print(f"Hessian Det: {det_H.evalf():.4f}")  # > 0
    print(f"Supremum: {max_lim}")         # 16/27


if __name__ == "__main__":
    verify_mass_gap()

Three things are worth highlighting about this check:

  • Gauge fixing. The three “anchor” null points \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3\) are placed at \(e(0,0,1)\), \(e(1,0,1)\), \(e(0,1,1)\) under the stereographic parametrization \(e(r,s,u) = \big[2ru : 2su : u^2-r^2-s^2 : u^2+r^2+s^2\big]\), which lands on the null quadric \(Q=0\) identically for any \((r,s,u)\). This is the \(\mathbb{P}^3\) analogue of fixing \(t_1=-1, t_2=0, t_3=1\) in the earlier planar post.
  • Stationarity and convexity. Evaluating the orthic quadrea at \(r=s=(\sqrt{3}-1)/2\) (with \(u=1\)) gives exactly \(5/54\), and the Hessian determinant there is positive, confirming a genuine local minimum rather than a saddle — consistent with the strictly convex bowl seen in Figures 4 and 5.
  • The degeneration limit. Taking \(s \to r\) and then \(r \to u\) pushes the fourth vertex toward the plane spanned by the other three; the symbolic limit of \(A_{\text{orthic}}\) under this substitution evaluates to exactly \(16/27\), matching the planar theorem from the earlier post without any numerical approximation.

Because every step — the orthocenter construction, the quadrea formula, the gauge fixing, and the limit — is carried out with exact rational and radical symbolic arithmetic, the computation is a verification of the algebra rather than a numerical approximation to it.

Discussion

Read together, the two posts describe a small hierarchy of absolute and extremal invariants that UHG’s orthocenter construction produces from null configurations:

  • Four null points in \(\mathbb{P}^2\) (a null quadrangle) give an orthic quadrangle whose diagonal triangle has a single, absolute quadrea, \(16/27\), independent of the configuration’s cross-ratio.
  • Four null points in \(\mathbb{P}^3\) (a null tetrahedron) give an orthic tetrahedron whose common face quadrea is no longer constant, but is confined to the range \([5/54,\,16/27)\) — a strict floor attained at a unique symmetric configuration, and a ceiling recovered exactly as the planar limit of the 2D result.

The fact that the \(\mathbb{P}^3\) supremum reproduces the \(\mathbb{P}^2\) constant on the nose, purely through a degeneration limit, is a strong internal consistency check that the two constructions are genuinely the same orthocenter machinery viewed one projective dimension apart. It also suggests a natural next question: does a null 5-point configuration in \(\mathbb{P}^4\) produce an analogous range, with the \(\mathbb{P}^3\) mass gap \(5/54\) appearing as its degeneration limit?

Conclusion

Starting from a null tetrahedron in \(\mathbb{P}^3\) — four points on the absolute quadric with no intrinsic metric of their own — the facewise orthocenter construction produces an orthic tetrahedron whose common face quadrea \(A_{\text{orthic}}\) is a well-defined projective invariant. Rather than being an absolute constant, as in the planar \(\mathbb{P}^2\) case, this invariant is confined to the range \([5/54, 16/27)\): a strict, uniquely attained lower bound at the orthically regular configuration — the orthic mass gap — and a supremum at \(16/27\) recovered exactly as the planar-degeneration limit of the earlier \(\mathbb{P}^2\) result. Both endpoints are confirmed by exact symbolic computation, with the Hessian at the minimum confirming a genuine, stable, strictly convex ground state rather than a coincidental stationary point.
