The question behind the paper

Electromagnetism can be described in several mathematical languages. In the familiar continuum approach, the electromagnetic potential is connected to local gauge symmetry. In lattice gauge theory, spacetime is represented by a discrete grid, and group-valued variables assigned to links between lattice sites describe how fields are compared from one site to another.

My research asks a related but distinct question: how much of this structure can be represented using exact algebraic and rational-geometric operations, without assuming that every quantity begins as a real-valued field?

The paper, From Local Gauge Symmetry to Exact Rational Geometry: A Structural Audit of a Finitist Rotor Framework Against Abelian Lattice QED, examines this question by comparing three things:

  1. the standard continuum presentation of Abelian local gauge symmetry;
  2. established constructions from lattice quantum electrodynamics (lattice QED); and
  3. a developing projective rotor framework intended to work with rational expressions and, where appropriate, other fields.

The goal is not to declare these approaches equivalent. It is to examine specific mathematical claims one by one, determine what can be established exactly, and identify what remains open.

Why make this comparison?

A mathematical framework can resemble a physical theory in its notation while differing from it in essential ways. For example, having a quantity that transforms like a gauge link is not by itself enough to establish a complete gauge theory. One must also account for matter fields, curvature, gauge-invariant quantities, dynamics, constraints, and the relationship between the mathematical construction and physical observables.

That is why the paper is organized as a structural audit rather than as a claim that rational geometry has replaced QED. The comparison asks where the constructions agree, under which hypotheses they agree, and where an apparent correspondence stops.

A quick guide to the main ideas

1. Local gauge symmetry and lattice links

In a lattice gauge theory, the basic geometric data include variables attached to links between neighbouring sites. In an Abelian theory, a link variable is often written (U_(x)). It transports a charged field from one lattice site to another and changes under a local gauge transformation at its endpoints.

The rotor framework studies link-like objects built from rational, Cayley-type expressions. The intended advantage is algebraic exactness: the operations can be expressed through field operations and matrix or projective constructions, rather than relying at every stage on trigonometric parameterizations.

But there is an important distinction. A rational expression that behaves like a phase is not automatically the same thing as a conventional complex phase (e^{i}), nor does it automatically reproduce every feature of the usual gauge theory. The transformation law and the composition rule have to be checked directly.

The paper therefore treats the link variables as geometric data in their own right and asks what follows from their algebraic structure.

2. Curvature from a discrete commutator

In continuum gauge theory, curvature measures the failure of covariant derivatives to commute. On a lattice, the corresponding geometric information is encoded by transport around a small closed loop, usually called a plaquette.

The paper derives a discrete commutator–plaquette identity of the schematic form

[ [,] = (U_P-1),_{}, ]

where (U_P) is the product of link variables around a plaquette and (_{}) is a corner-transport factor determined by the chosen forward-difference convention.

The corner factor matters: with discrete forward differences, the two paths being compared do not necessarily act at the same site in the same way as their continuum analogues. Writing the factor explicitly makes the identity precise rather than relying on a loose analogy.

The takeaway is modest but useful: the relation between a discrete commutator and plaquette transport can be written as an exact algebraic identity. This is a mathematical result about the specified lattice operators; it is not, by itself, a derivation of the full dynamics of electromagnetism.

3. Why an isolated link cannot carry a nontrivial gauge-invariant potential

Another result concerns a single link. Under a local Abelian gauge transformation, the link changes according to the gauge factors at its two endpoints. Because those endpoint factors can vary independently, the gauge orbit of an isolated link is transitive under the stated assumptions.

Consequently, a function of that link alone that is invariant under all such transformations must be constant. In other words, there is no nontrivial gauge-invariant potential depending on an isolated link alone in this setting.

This is consistent with a familiar feature of gauge theory: gauge-invariant information is generally associated with combinations such as closed-loop products, or with matter and link variables combined in suitable ways, rather than with a bare link by itself.

The conclusion is deliberately scoped to an isolated link and the transformations specified in the paper. It should not be read as a general statement that no gauge-invariant terms can be constructed in a lattice theory.

4. The Wilson–Dirac operator and fermion doubling

A lattice discretization of fermions faces a well-known problem called fermion doubling: a straightforward discretization can produce additional low-energy modes beyond the intended fermion content. The Wilson–Dirac operator introduces an additional term designed to control these unwanted modes.

The paper analyzes the free Wilson–Dirac operator in a specified four-dimensional periodic lattice setting, including an (L=4) calculation over the quadratic extension ((i)). The determinant calculation uses the algebra of the gamma matrices and the momentum-dependent operator to establish the claimed result in that setting.

This is a finite-lattice algebraic calculation, not a universal theorem about every lattice size, every field, or every interacting theory. Over finite fields, the survival or removal of particular modes becomes an arithmetic question: the relevant momentum values and denominators behave differently depending on the field.

The broader point is methodological. Some questions usually discussed using analytic or numerical tools can also be examined through exact finite-dimensional algebra—provided the scope of the calculation is stated clearly.

5. The rotor anchor and electromagnetic invariants

The paper also considers the complex Riemann–Silberstein field

[ =+i, ]

which packages the electric and magnetic fields into one complex vector. Its bilinear product is

[ = (2-2) +2i,. ]

The real and imaginary parts encode the two familiar quadratic electromagnetic invariants (up to the conventional choice of units and signs).

The rotor-anchor construction is intended to provide an algebraic representation of this packaging. The identity above is exact once the complex vector is defined. What requires separate justification is the larger claim that a proposed rotor construction reproduces all of the transformation laws, field equations, and physical content of electromagnetism. The paper distinguishes the invariant identity from that broader physical question.

6. A no-go result for additive parameters and rational Cayley phases

One of the paper’s central limitations is also one of its useful findings.

In the usual continuum description, a gauge potential is Lie-algebra-valued, and link variables are commonly related to it using an exponential map. The rotor framework instead investigates rational Cayley-type phase composition.

The paper proves a no-go result under specified algebraic hypotheses: there is no nontrivial group homomorphism from the additive group ((,+)), or from the stated finite-field additive groups ((_p,+)), into the rational Cayley phase group ((^1,)), for the field conditions given in the manuscript.

In practical terms, this means that one cannot simply assume that an additive potential can be converted into rational Cayley links by a map that preserves the required group composition exactly. The theorem blocks that particular route.

It does not prove that every possible link construction is impossible, that continuum potentials are invalid, or that ordinary lattice QED fails. Its conclusion is narrower: under the theorem’s hypotheses, the desired additive-to-Cayley homomorphism does not exist. A rational lattice theory must therefore specify its group-valued link data directly or use a construction that does not violate those hypotheses.

A no-go theorem can be productive because it identifies which strategy cannot work and clarifies what a viable construction must do differently.

7. Discrete dynamics and the Gauss constraint

The paper goes beyond static geometry to examine a coupled (2+1)-dimensional lattice scheme with rational Cayley updates. It gives explicit equations for the scalar and gauge sectors and proves algebraic statements about charge continuity and preservation of a discrete Gauss constraint.

The distinction between exact algebra and numerical behaviour is important here. An identity showing that a constraint is preserved by the symbolic update does not, on its own, establish numerical stability under finite-precision computation. Likewise, a reversible rational update is not automatically an exact splitting of the particular Hamiltonian written beside it.

The paper treats these points separately. The stated Gauss-preservation and continuity results are algebraic claims about the defined update, while stability and exact Hamiltonian correspondence require their own analysis.

What the paper establishes—and what it does not

It helps to separate the results into categories.

Topic What is established in the stated setting What is not thereby established
Discrete curvature An exact commutator–plaquette identity, including the corner transporter A complete electromagnetic field theory
Single-link gauge orbit No nonconstant gauge-invariant potential of an isolated link under the specified transformations A prohibition on all gauge-invariant lattice interactions
Wilson–Dirac operator A finite-dimensional algebraic result for the stated free, periodic-lattice setup A universal result for all lattices and interacting fermions
Rotor anchor A bilinear identity encoding the two quadratic electromagnetic invariants Full physical equivalence with Maxwell theory or QED
Rational phase map A no-go theorem for an additive-to-Cayley homomorphism under stated hypotheses Impossibility of every conceivable link construction
Discrete dynamics Algebraic charge continuity and Gauss-constraint preservation for the specified scheme Numerical stability or exact splitting of the displayed Hamiltonian

This separation is central to the paper’s purpose. Exact identities deserve to be stated as exact identities. Finite-lattice calculations should be identified as such. Analogies should not be presented as proofs of physical equivalence.

Why a finitist or rational-geometric approach?

The word finitist here signals an interest in constructions that can be expressed through explicit algebraic operations, rational maps, and finite-dimensional data. The goal is not simply to replace one notation with another. It is to investigate which parts of the geometry can be built and verified without taking analytic structures for granted.

This perspective can be useful for several reasons:

  • Exact verification: identities can sometimes be checked symbolically rather than inferred from numerical approximations.
  • Field sensitivity: working over () or finite fields makes arithmetic assumptions visible instead of hiding them inside real or complex analysis.
  • Structural clarity: no-go results can reveal which ingredients a construction must treat as fundamental.
  • Reproducible scope: a result can be tied to explicit operators, lattice sizes, fields, and hypotheses.

These benefits come with constraints. Some standard physical tools rely on analytic, topological, or limiting arguments that do not automatically carry over to a rational setting. Any replacement has to show how the relevant structure is represented and what is preserved.

The main open problem

The paper does not claim to have constructed a complete alternative to lattice QED. A key unresolved task is to couple dynamical Dirac fermions consistently to the exact discrete Gauss constraint within the proposed framework.

More broadly, a complete physical comparison would need to address the dynamical matter sector, the appropriate action or evolution principle, the observables, and the conditions under which the algebraic construction reproduces the target theory’s physical content. Those are substantive requirements, not details that can be settled by a matching formula alone.

For that reason, the paper is best read as a mathematical audit and a research map: it records specific exact results, identifies limitations, and points to the additional work needed before a stronger equivalence claim could be made.

Conclusion

The central message is simple: rational geometry offers a framework in which some gauge-theoretic structures can be expressed and tested exactly, but exact algebraic correspondence is not the same thing as full physical equivalence.

The paper makes this distinction concrete through discrete curvature, gauge-orbit structure, a finite Wilson–Dirac calculation, electromagnetic invariants, a rational equivariance no-go theorem, and a Gauss-preserving discrete dynamical scheme.

The positive results show that meaningful pieces of the comparison can be carried out explicitly. The limitations are equally important: they prevent a local identity or a finite calculation from being mistaken for a complete theory. The next stage is not to overstate what has been achieved, but to solve the remaining structural problems—especially the consistent coupling of dynamical fermions—and then test precisely what physical conclusions follow.
