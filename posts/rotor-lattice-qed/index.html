The starting point: Flournoy’s Lecture 13

In high-energy physics, the standard way students first learn that forces arise from symmetries is through a famous sequence of arguments. A particularly clean, transparent presentation of this is Alex Flournoy’s Particle Physics Lecture 13: Interactions via Local Gauge Symmetry (The Abelian Case) (handwritten lecture notes).

The lecture builds electrodynamics from scratch across four progressive steps: 1. Global phase symmetry: A free Dirac electron field \(\psi(x)\) is invariant under a constant phase rotation \(\psi \to e^{iq\phi}\psi\). 2. Local gauge symmetry & minimal coupling: If we demand invariance under a spacetime-dependent phase \(\phi(x)\), ordinary derivatives \(\partial_\mu \psi\) fail to transform covariantly. Restoring covariance forces us to replace \(\partial_\mu\) with the covariant derivative \(D_\mu = \partial_\mu + i q A_\mu(x)\). This automatically pulls in a compensating vector connection \(A_\mu(x)\) and generates the interaction term \(q j^\mu A_\mu\). 3. Masslessness of the photon: Appending an explicit Proca mass term \(m_A^2 A_\mu A^\mu\) breaks this local symmetry, requiring \(m_A = 0\). 4. Field strength as curvature: Commuting two covariant derivatives yields the electromagnetic field strength: \([D_\mu, D_\nu]\psi = i q F_{\mu\nu}\psi\).

For the past several weeks, our research programme has asked a foundational question: Can this entire pedagogical sequence be formulated on a discrete grid using purely rational, finitist geometry—without limits, transcendentals (\(\sin, \cos, \exp\)), or real-number completions?

To answer this, we conducted a comprehensive mathematical audit comparing Flournoy’s lecture, standard Lattice Quantum Electrodynamics (QED), and our projective \(2\times 2\) rotor/Cayley framework.

The completed paper is now archived on Zenodo: > John D. Coady, From Local Gauge Symmetry to Exact Rational Geometry: A Structural Audit of a Finitist Rotor Framework Against Abelian Lattice QED, Preprint (October 2026), DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.23605833.

This post outlines the audit: where the rational rotor framework matches standard gauge theory exactly, what mathematical no-go theorem defines its boundary, and what remains open.

Four Exact Mathematical Passes

Four major results in the audit stand out as exact theorems holding without approximations:

1. The Lattice Commutator and the Corner Transporter (Benchmark 7)

In standard textbooks, the lattice commutator of covariant derivatives is said to approach \(i q a^2 F_{\mu\nu}\psi\) only in the continuum limit \(a \to 0\). In our paper, we prove an exact finite-spacing algebraic identity: \[ [\mathcal{D}_\mu, \mathcal{D}_\nu]\psi(x) = \bigl(U_P(x) - 1\bigr)\,\mathcal{T}_{\mu\nu}\psi(x), \] where \(U_P(x)\) is the oriented product of the four links around the plaquette, and \[ \mathcal{T}_{\mu\nu}\psi(x) \equiv U_\nu(x) U_\mu(x + \hat\nu)\,\psi(x + \hat\mu + \hat\nu) \] is the matter field parallel-transported from the far corner of the plaquette back to site \(x\). * The identity shows that the lattice curvature naturally acts on a transported field rather than a purely local field. * When \(U_P \ne -1\), the scalar Cayley curvature parameter is extracted without dividing spinors: \[ a_P = i\,\frac{U_P - 1}{U_P + 1}. \]

2. The Single-Link Transitivity Theorem (Benchmark 6)

Why can’t a gauge field have a bare mass? In Flournoy’s lecture, a Proca mass term \(m_A^2 A_\mu A^\mu\) is ruled out because \(A_\mu \to A_\mu - \partial_\mu \phi\) is not invariant.

On the lattice, we prove a non-perturbative analogue: > Theorem: Independent gauge transformations at the two endpoints of a link act transitively on the link’s projective Cayley parameter \(a \in \mathbb{P}^1(F)\).

Because any link can be transformed into any other link by an endpoint gauge choice, any gauge-invariant potential depending on an isolated link alone must be constant. This forbids a bare mass potential on links while naturally leaving room for gauge-invariant plaquettes and electric kinetic terms \(\frac{1}{2}E^2\).

3. Wilson–Dirac Zero Modes on an \(L=4\) Lattice (Benchmark 1)

To represent the electron, we set up chiral Euclidean gamma matrices with entries in the quadratic extension \(K = F[i]\). * For the naive lattice Dirac operator (\(r=0\)), evaluating the operator on an \(L=4\) grid reveals the infamous fermion doubling problem: the determinant vanishes at all 16 corners of the Brillouin zone, producing 64 unphysical zero modes (16 corners \(\times\) 4 spinor components). * For the Wilson operator (\(r=1\)) over the Gaussian rationals \(\mathbb{Q}(i)\), the determinant formula: \[ \det\mathcal{D}_W(k) = \bigl(M(k)^2 + n_{\mathrm{odd}}\bigr)^2 \] forces \(k = (0,0,0,0)\). All 15 doubler species are strictly eliminated! * Over finite fields \(\mathbb{F}_p\) (\(p \equiv 3 \pmod 4\)), doubler elimination turns into a modular congruence: \((n_{\mathrm{odd}} + 2n_2)^2 \equiv -n_{\mathrm{odd}} \pmod p\). For \(p=7\), accidental zero modes survive (33 singular modes), while for \(p=11\), only \(k=0\) survives, matching \(\mathbb{Q}\).

4. The Rotor Anchor Packages Both Field Invariants (Benchmark 8)

When the \(4\times 4\) rotor anchor matrix \(A(\zeta)\) is parametrized by \(\zeta = \kappa + i\rho \in K^3\), identifying \(\kappa = \mathbf{E}\) and \(\rho = \mathbf{B}\) identifies \(\zeta\) with the classical Riemann–Silberstein vector. Its quadratic scalar decomposes into: \[ \zeta \cdot \zeta = (\mathbf{E}^2 - \mathbf{B}^2) + 2i(\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}). \] The real part is the Maxwell Lagrangian density \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{EM}}\), and the imaginary part is the topological \(\theta\)-term / axion coupling density.

The Core Boundary: The Equivariance No-Go Theorem

The most important theoretical insight in the paper is Theorem 4.1 (The Rational Equivariance No-Go Theorem).

In Lattice QED, link variables are defined from continuum vector potentials via the exponential map: \[ U_e = \exp\left(iq \int_e \mathbf{A}\cdot d\boldsymbol{\ell}\right). \] Under \(\mathbf{A} \to \mathbf{A} + \nabla\lambda\), the fundamental theorem of calculus ensures that \(U_e\) transforms with exact equivariance: \(U_e \to e^{iq\lambda(x)} U_e e^{-iq\lambda(y)}\).

Can we do this in rational algebra? Can we find a rational map \(a_e(\mathbf{A}) \in \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q})\) such that: \[ a_e(\mathbf{A} + \nabla\lambda) = a_e(\mathbf{A}) \oplus \gamma(\lambda(x)) \ominus \gamma(\lambda(y))? \]

The answer is an absolute NO.

We prove that there are no non-trivial group homomorphisms: \[ \mathrm{Hom}\bigl((\mathbb{Q}, +),\, (\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q}), \oplus)\bigr) = \{0\}, \] and similarly for finite fields: \[ \mathrm{Hom}\bigl((\mathbb{F}_p, +),\, (\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{F}_p), \oplus)\bigr) = \{0\} \quad (p \equiv 3 \pmod 4). \]

The Sketch of the Proof

The Cayley group \((\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q}), \oplus)\) is isomorphic to the multiplicative group of norm-one elements \(G = \{z \in \mathbb{Q}(i)^\times : z\bar z = 1\}\). 1. The additive group \((\mathbb{Q}, +)\) is divisible: for any rational \(x\) and any integer \(n\), \(x/n \in \mathbb{Q}\). 2. Therefore, any homomorphic image in \(G\) must be divisible: every element \(g\) must have an \(n\)-th root in the image for every integer \(n\). 3. In the Gaussian integer ring \(\mathbb{Z}[i]\) (a unique factorization domain), prime ideal valuations \(v_\pi(g)\) must be divisible by all \(n\), forcing all valuations to be zero. 4. Thus, \(g\) must be a Gaussian unit: \(g \in \{\pm 1, \pm i\} = \mu_4\). 5. But the finite group \(\mu_4\) contains no non-trivial divisible subgroups. Therefore, the homomorphism must be identically trivial!

Why this No-Go Theorem is an Asset

The Wu–Yang Insight: Why Links Are More Fundamental Than Potentials

Why should we be comfortable abandoning continuous vector potentials \(\mathbf{A}\) in favor of primary links \(U\)?

In a landmark 1975 paper, Tai Tsun Wu and Chen Ning Yang famously analyzed the Aharonov–Bohm effect to determine what the true, intrinsic mathematical descriptor of electromagnetism actually is (Wu & Yang, Phys. Rev. D 12, 3845 (1975)). They concluded: 1. The field strength \(F_{\mu\nu}\) is an under-description of nature: If you only know \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\), you cannot explain the Aharonov–Bohm effect (where electrons experience physical phase shifts in regions where \(\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{B} = 0\)). 2. The vector potential \(A_\mu\) is an over-description of nature: \(A_\mu\) contains vast amounts of unphysical gauge ambiguity. 3. The true, minimal, complete description of electromagnetism is the non-integrable Phase Factor (the Link / Holonomy): \[U = \mathcal{P}\exp\left(iq \oint A_\mu dx^\mu\right)\]

In 19th-century physics, we started with continuous potentials \(A_\mu\) because calculus required smooth lines. But quantum geometry teaches us that the phase factor on a path is the primary reality.

Theorem 4.1 provides an exact algebraic proof of Wu and Yang’s thesis: you cannot smoothly force group-valued phase factors to be projections of additive potentials without breaking exact rational equivariance. Taking the group links as primary data is exactly what gauge theory demanded all along.

This theorem is not a failure; it is a structural clarification: * It proves mathematically why standard lattice gauge theory must use the transcendental exponential map \(\exp(iq\int A)\) if it wants continuous potentials to transform links equivariantly. * In a purely rational or finitist framework, gauge links cannot be derived from a continuous Lie-algebra 1-form \(\mathbf{A}\). * The Takeaway: In rational gauge theory, group-valued link variables must be taken as primary geometric data, not as approximations of a continuum vector field.

Exact Coupled Dynamics and the Open Frontier

Finally, the paper analyzes the dynamical sector (Benchmark 9).

In Parts 7–9 of our blog series, we developed an explicit, reversible, symmetric Kick–Cayley-Drift–Kick scheme on a \(2+1\)D grid for compact \(U(1)\) links coupled to a complex scalar field \(\phi\): * Kick: Updates electric fields \(E\) and scalar momenta \(\pi\) using algebraic forces derived from the plaquette potential and the gauge-matter hopping term. * Drift: Updates the links via Cayley tangent addition \(a \leftarrow a \oplus (-\frac{h}{2}E)\), using homogeneous projective coordinates \((x_0 : x_1) \mapsto (x_0 + \frac{h}{2}E x_1 : x_1 - \frac{h}{2}E x_0)\) to handle \(a = \infty\) without singular denominators.

Exact Gauss Preservation

We prove that: \[ \Delta(\mathrm{div}\,E) = -h\,\mathrm{div}J, \qquad \Delta\rho = +h\,\mathrm{div}J, \] so that the Gauss constraint functional \(G(x) \equiv (\mathrm{div}\,E)(x) + \rho(x)\) satisfies: \[ G_{\mathrm{after}}(x) = G_{\mathrm{before}}(x) \quad \text{identically}. \] In numerical simulations over 20,000 steps, \(\max|G|\) remains strictly at floating-point machine precision (\(\sim 10^{-15}\)). Over \(\mathbb{Q}\) and \(\mathbb{F}_p\), the residual is literally 0.

What Remains Open: The Fermion-to-Gauss Coupling

In Table 1, Row 9 remains classified as a Conditional Match for one clear reason: our working dynamical code uses a bosonic scalar field \(\phi\), whereas Flournoy’s lecture specifies a Dirac fermion \(\psi\).

In computational field theory, Dirac fermions cannot be treated as classical numbers in a leapfrog update; they are anticommuting operators (or Grassmann fields).

This defines the primary open problem of our programme: > The Challenge: Formulate a discrete Hamiltonian time step coupling the free Wilson–Dirac operator (Benchmark 1) to the Cayley electric field \(E\), such that the fermionic Gauss constraint \((\mathrm{div}\,E)(x) = q\,\psi^\dagger(x)\psi(x)\) and fermionic current continuity are preserved identically by the algebraic update.

Summary: A Clear Map of the Territory

By benchmarking our rational rotor framework against Flournoy’s Lecture 13 and Lattice QED, we have replaced speculation with a rigorous audit:

  1. Exact Kinematics: The corner-transporter commutator \([\mathcal{D}_\mu, \mathcal{D}_\nu]\psi = (U_P - 1)\mathcal{T}_{\mu\nu}\psi\), the single-link transitivity theorem, and the Wilson doubler elimination over \(\mathbb{Q}(i)\) are mathematically proved at finite lattice spacing.
  2. The Rational Boundary: The Equivariance No-Go Theorem proves that rational gauge theory must treat group-valued links as primary variables rather than projections of continuous vector potentials.
  3. The Clear Next Step: Having established exact classical scalar dynamics with zero Gauss drift, the immediate research challenge is coupling dynamical Dirac fermions to the discrete Gauss law.
