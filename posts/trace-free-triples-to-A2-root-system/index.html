From Trace-Free Triples to the A2 Root System: Making the SU(3) Analogy Precise

Picking up where we left off

This is the fourth post in the series exploring the algebra hiding inside a very simple picture: four null points on a conic, and the seven-point figure their diagonal triangle generates.

  • Post 1 (Reciprocal Parallax Symmetry in UHG) introduced the four null points, their self-polar diagonal triangle, and the rigid, cross-ratio-independent quadrance of the diagonal triangle’s sides.
  • Post 2 (Parallax Diagonal Triangulation) triangulated that diagonal triangle outward and found the six outer parallax quadrances tracing the full anharmonic \(S_3\) orbit of a cross-ratio \(q\).
  • Post 3 (From Cross-Ratio Orbits to SU(3) Invariants) folded that six-element orbit down onto Wildberger’s 48/64 locus, and separately found that the central triangle \(\overline{a_2a_4a_6}\) has side-quadrances \(q_1(q), q_2(q), q_3(q)\) satisfying a trace-free condition \(q_1+q_2+q_3\equiv 0\) — the same shape as the Cartan subalgebra of \(\mathfrak{su}(3)\). That post was deliberately cautious: “the SU(3) language is offered as structural motivation, not as a physical derivation.”

This post closes most of that gap. The trace-free triple isn’t just shaped like a Cartan-subalgebra element — the \(S_3\) symmetry acting on it is, exactly and checkably, the permutation action that defines the \(A_2\) root system. From there, two classical theorems (Schur’s lemma and Serre’s theorem) do the rest of the work, with no additional geometric input required.

A note on notation, adopted from this point in the series onward: capital \(Q\) denotes a Rational Trigonometry quadrance — the raw value of the ambient quadratic form on a single homogeneous representative, \(Q(v)=x^2+y^2-z^2\), which is not invariant under choice of representative. Lowercase \(q\) denotes a UHG quadrance — the projectively invariant ratio \(q(u,v) = 1 - B(u,v)^2/(Q(u)Q(v))\) between two points. Everything in this post is built from the latter: \(q_1, q_2, q_3\) below are UHG quadrances between pairs of central-triangle vertices, never raw RT values of a single constructed point. This distinction turns out to matter a great deal — later posts in the series ran into a real, instructive error by blurring it — and every result here is safe from that issue precisely because it never leaves the world of scale-invariant ratios.

Where \((q_1, q_2, q_3)\) actually lives

Recall the central-triangle quadrances from Post 3:

\[ q_1(q) = \frac{q^2-q+1}{q}, \qquad q_2(q) = \frac{q^2-q+1}{q(q-1)}, \qquad q_3(q) = -\frac{q^2-q+1}{q-1}, \]

with \(q_1+q_2+q_3\equiv 0\) identically. That single linear identity means that, for every \(q\), the point \((q_1(q), q_2(q), q_3(q))\) lies in the plane

\[ \mathfrak{h} = \{(x_1,x_2,x_3)\in\mathbb{R}^3 : x_1+x_2+x_3=0\} \subset \mathbb{R}^3. \]

This is not a cosmetic observation. \(\mathfrak{h}\) is exactly the standard model of the Cartan subalgebra of \(\mathfrak{sl}_3\): the traceless diagonal \(3\times 3\) matrices, or equivalently the plane orthogonal to \((1,1,1)\) in the weight space spanned by \(e_1, e_2, e_3\). The question is not whether \((q_1,q_2,q_3)\) fits in this plane — trivially, any three numbers summing to zero do. The question is whether the symmetry acting on it matches the symmetry that makes \(\mathfrak{h}\) carry the \(A_2\) root system. It does.

The permutation dictionary

The anharmonic group of a cross-ratio is generated by two involutions, \(q\mapsto 1/q\) and \(q\mapsto 1-q\), together with their composite \(q \mapsto q/(q-1)\), giving the full six-element group \(S_3\). Substituting each of these into \(q_1, q_2, q_3\) and simplifying yields three exact rational identities:

\[ \begin{aligned} q \mapsto 1/q: &\quad q_1 \text{ fixed}, \quad q_2 \leftrightarrow q_3 \\ q \mapsto 1-q: &\quad q_2 \text{ fixed}, \quad q_1 \leftrightarrow q_3 \\ q \mapsto q/(q-1): &\quad q_3 \text{ fixed}, \quad q_1 \leftrightarrow q_2 \end{aligned} \]

Each line is an identity in \(q\), verified symbolically below — not a numerical check at a sample point. This means the abstract \(S_3\) generated by these three Möbius maps acts on \((q_1, q_2, q_3)\) by literally permuting three labeled coordinates. That is precisely how \(S_3\) acts on the weight vectors \(e_1, e_2, e_3\) of the defining representation of \(\mathfrak{sl}_3\), whose differences \(e_i - e_j\) are the six roots of \(A_2\).

Why this is enough: Schur’s lemma

The standard 2-dimensional real representation of \(S_3\) — permutation of three coordinates, restricted to the trace-free plane \(\mathfrak{h}\) — is irreducible. By Schur’s lemma, any \(S_3\)-invariant symmetric bilinear form on \(\mathfrak{h}\) is unique up to an overall scale. The ordinary dot product on \(\mathbb{R}^3\), restricted to \(\mathfrak{h}\), is manifestly invariant under coordinate permutation, so it is that unique form (up to scale).

Since \((q_1, q_2, q_3)\) transforms under the verified dictionary above exactly as \(S_3\) permuting coordinates, its six-point orbit under the full anharmonic group is, for every generic \(q\), a regular hexagon in \(\mathfrak{h}\) — not approximately, and not merely “hexagon-shaped” by loose analogy, but forced to be a scaled copy of the \(A_2\) root hexagon by the representation-theoretic argument above. No coordinate choice or extra geometric input is needed to get there.

Reading off the Cartan matrix

Take the transpositions for \(q\mapsto q/(q-1)\) and \(q\mapsto 1/q\) as the two simple reflections, with corresponding roots

\[ \alpha_1 = e_1 - e_2, \qquad \alpha_2 = e_2 - e_3. \]

The ordinary dot product gives \((\alpha_1,\alpha_1) = (\alpha_2,\alpha_2) = 2\) and \((\alpha_1,\alpha_2) = -1\), and the Cartan matrix formula \(A_{ij} = 2(\alpha_i,\alpha_j)/(\alpha_j,\alpha_j)\) returns exactly

\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2\end{pmatrix}. \]

One subtlety is worth flagging explicitly, since it is easy to get backwards: the third transposition, \(q\mapsto 1-q\), corresponds to the root \(e_1 - e_3 = \alpha_1+\alpha_2\) — the highest root, not a second simple root. Pairing a simple root with the highest root instead of with the other simple root changes the sign of the resulting inner product (\(+1\) instead of \(-1\)), since the angle involved is \(60°\) rather than \(120°\). All three transpositions generate \(S_3\) identically well, but only an adjacent pair of them reproduces the textbook \(A_2\) Cartan matrix directly from inner products.

From Cartan matrix to Lie algebra: Serre’s theorem

This is the step that upgrades the result from “matches the invariant theory of \(\mathfrak{su}(3)\)” to “is, by construction, the \(A_2\) Cartan matrix, and therefore the \(\mathfrak{sl}_3(\mathbb{C})\)/\(\mathfrak{su}(3)\) root data.” The Chevalley–Serre construction states that any valid Cartan matrix determines a unique (up to isomorphism) complex semisimple Lie algebra, generated by Chevalley triples subject to the Serre relations built from that matrix. Feeding in \(A\) above, the Cartan–Killing classification identifies the result unambiguously as \(\mathfrak{sl}_3(\mathbb{C})\), whose compact real form is \(\mathfrak{su}(3)\).

So the precise claim is: the central triangle’s trace-free quadrances realize the \(A_2\) root system as a geometric object (Schur’s lemma), and by Serre’s theorem the semisimple Lie algebra with that root system is \(\mathfrak{sl}_3(\mathbb{C})\)/\(\mathfrak{su}(3)\) — not “SU(3)-flavored,” but that specific isomorphism class.

An explicit matrix picture

The abstract argument above has a fully concrete matrix realization. Embed the central-triangle data directly as a traceless diagonal matrix,

\[ H(q) = \mathrm{diag}\big(q_1(q),\, q_2(q),\, q_3(q)\big), \]

which lies in the Cartan subalgebra of \(\mathfrak{sl}_3(\mathbb{C})\) by construction, since \(\operatorname{tr} H(q) = 0\). Conjugating \(H(q)\) by permutation matrices reproduces exactly the transpositions verified above. The adjoint action of \(H(q)\) on the standard root vectors \(E_{ij}\) (elementary off-diagonal matrices) has eigenvalues \(q_i(q) - q_j(q)\) — these are the \(A_2\) roots evaluated at the specific Cartan element \(H(q)\). The characteristic polynomial of \(H(q)\) recovers the same Casimirs \(C_2, C_3\) already tied to the Klein \(j\)-invariant in Post 3, now visibly as the elementary symmetric functions of \(H(q)\)’s eigenvalues.

Note that \(H(q)\) is built entirely from \(q_1, q_2, q_3\) — UHG quadrances, scale-invariant ratios — and never from a raw RT value \(Q(v)\) of an individually constructed homogeneous point. That distinction is what keeps this construction on solid ground.

Check it yourself

from sympy import symbols, simplify

q = symbols('q')

q1 = (q**2 - q + 1) / q
q2 = (q**2 - q + 1) / (q*(q - 1))
q3 = -(q**2 - q + 1) / (q - 1)

def sub(expr, qval):
    return simplify(expr.subs(q, qval))

# q -> 1/q : fixes q1, swaps q2 <-> q3
print("q1(1/q) - q1(q)      =", simplify(sub(q1, 1/q) - q1))
print("q2(1/q) - q3(q)      =", simplify(sub(q2, 1/q) - q3))
print("q3(1/q) - q2(q)      =", simplify(sub(q3, 1/q) - q2))

# q -> 1-q : fixes q2, swaps q1 <-> q3
print("q2(1-q) - q2(q)      =", simplify(sub(q2, 1-q) - q2))
print("q1(1-q) - q3(q)      =", simplify(sub(q1, 1-q) - q3))
print("q3(1-q) - q1(q)      =", simplify(sub(q3, 1-q) - q1))

# q -> q/(q-1) : fixes q3, swaps q1 <-> q2
qp = q/(q-1)
print("q3(q/(q-1)) - q3(q)  =", simplify(sub(q3, qp) - q3))
print("q1(q/(q-1)) - q2(q)  =", simplify(sub(q1, qp) - q2))
print("q2(q/(q-1)) - q1(q)  =", simplify(sub(q2, qp) - q1))

# Cartan matrix from adjacent simple roots
from sympy import Matrix
e1, e2, e3 = Matrix([1,0,0]), Matrix([0,1,0]), Matrix([0,0,1])
a1, a2 = e1 - e2, e2 - e3
print("\n(a1,a1) =", a1.dot(a1), " (a2,a2) =", a2.dot(a2), " (a1,a2) =", a1.dot(a2))
q1(1/q) - q1(q)      = 0
q2(1/q) - q3(q)      = 0
q3(1/q) - q2(q)      = 0
q2(1-q) - q2(q)      = 0
q1(1-q) - q3(q)      = 0
q3(1-q) - q1(q)      = 0
q3(q/(q-1)) - q3(q)  = 0
q1(q/(q-1)) - q2(q)  = 0
q2(q/(q-1)) - q1(q)  = 0

(a1,a1) = 2  (a2,a2) = 2  (a1,a2) = -1

Every line is a symbolic zero — an identity in \(q\), holding across the whole domain, exactly as in the earlier posts.

Two things this result does not establish

It is worth being as precise about the boundary of this result as about its content.

The image is a curve, not the whole Cartan plane. As \(q\) ranges over \(\mathbb{P}^1\), \((q_1(q), q_2(q), q_3(q))\) traces a one-dimensional curve inside the two-dimensional plane \(\mathfrak{h}\) — the null quadrangle, up to projective equivalence, has exactly one modulus. The construction produces a distinguished, \(S_3\)-invariant curve inside the Cartan plane, not every possible Cartan-subalgebra element.

The ambient spaces are not unified. The diagonal and central triangles live in the projective (equivalently, affine, via \([x:y:z]\mapsto(x/z,y/z)\)) plane of Universal Hyperbolic Geometry, sitting inside a 3-dimensional space equipped with a bilinear form — the quadratic form defining the conic, with UHG quadrance between points equal to rational-trigonometry spread between the corresponding lines through the origin. That is a genuinely different ambient 3-space, with a different symmetry group (isometries of the bilinear form), from the weight space \(\{e_1,e_2,e_3\}\) carrying the \(S_3\) permutation action. The bridge established here runs through the scalar cross-ratio \(q\) — a single real or complex modulus extracted from the UHG configuration — not through any map identifying the two ambient 3-spaces directly. Building such a map, one that intertwines the conic’s symmetry with the \(S_3\) permutation action, would be a materially stronger and separate claim.

Where this leaves us

Three posts of exact projective and rational-trigonometric algebra produced a trace-free triple with the right shape for a Cartan-subalgebra element. This post shows that the shape is not superficial: the anharmonic \(S_3\) acts on that triple by literal coordinate permutation, which by Schur’s lemma forces the orbit to realize the \(A_2\) root system geometrically, and by Serre’s theorem pins the resulting Lie algebra down as \(\mathfrak{sl}_3(\mathbb{C})\) — the complexification of \(\mathfrak{su}(3)\) — with no remaining appeal to analogy.

What hasn’t been done is to connect the ambient ternary quadratic form of UHG itself to the weight space of \(\mathfrak{su}(3)\)’s defining representation, or to find any actual representation of \(\mathfrak{su}(3)\) acting on the null-quadrangle configuration beyond this invariant-theoretic matching. That is the natural next question for the series.
